Chapter 5 • Question 7
First-Principles Derivation

Continuous Mixture Models

Two marginal (prior predictive) distributions arise from integrating a likelihood against a conjugate prior. Using only the laws of iterated expectation and total variance, we derive the mean and variance of the negative binomial distribution from a Poisson–gamma mixture, and the first two moments of the Student-$t$ posterior for a normal mean with unknown scale.

Exercise Description

  1. If $y \mid \omega \sim \text{Poisson}(\omega)$, and $\omega \sim \text{Gamma}(\alpha, \beta)$, then the marginal (prior predictive) distribution of $y$ is negative binomial with parameters $\alpha$ and $\beta$ (or $p = \beta / (1 + \beta)$). Use the formulas (2.7) and (2.8) to derive the mean and variance of the negative binomial.
  2. In the normal model with unknown location and scale $(\mu, \sigma^2)$, the noninformative prior density $p(\mu, \sigma^2) \propto 1/\sigma^2$ results in a normal-inverse-$\chi^2$ posterior distribution for $(\mu, \sigma^2)$. Marginally then $\sqrt{n}(\mu - \bar{y})/s$ has a posterior distribution that is $t_{n-1}$. Use (2.7) and (2.8) to derive the first two moments of the latter distribution, stating the appropriate condition on $n$ for existence of both moments.

0. The Two Tools

Both parts are solved with the same two identities. If $y$ and $\theta$ are jointly distributed, then the law of iterated expectation (formula 2.7) gives the marginal mean, and the law of total variance (formula 2.8) decomposes the marginal variance into an expected conditional variance plus the variance of the conditional mean:

$$\operatorname{E}(y) = \operatorname{E}\!\big(\operatorname{E}(y \mid \theta)\big) \tag{2.7}$$ $$\operatorname{var}(y) = \operatorname{E}\!\big(\operatorname{var}(y \mid \theta)\big) + \operatorname{var}\!\big(\operatorname{E}(y \mid \theta)\big) \tag{2.8}$$

The strategy in each part is to pick the right variable to condition on so that the inner conditional moments are known in closed form, and then average over the mixing variable.

1. Part A: The Poisson–Gamma Mixture

We condition on the rate $\omega$, which is the natural mixing variable. The two building blocks are the moments of the Poisson likelihood and of the gamma prior:

  • Poisson likelihood: $\operatorname{E}(y \mid \omega) = \omega$ and $\operatorname{var}(y \mid \omega) = \omega$ (the mean equals the variance).
  • Gamma prior (rate parametrization): $\operatorname{E}(\omega) = \dfrac{\alpha}{\beta}$ and $\operatorname{var}(\omega) = \dfrac{\alpha}{\beta^2}$.

The Mean

Applying (2.7) with $\theta = \omega$, the marginal mean is simply the prior mean of the rate:

$$\operatorname{E}(y) = \operatorname{E}\!\big(\operatorname{E}(y \mid \omega)\big) = \operatorname{E}(\omega) = \frac{\alpha}{\beta}$$

The Variance

Applying (2.8), both terms are non-trivial. The expected conditional variance is $\operatorname{E}(\omega)$, while the variance of the conditional mean is $\operatorname{var}(\omega)$. The mixture therefore adds the extra dispersion of the prior on top of the Poisson variability:

$$\operatorname{var}(y) = \underbrace{\operatorname{E}\!\big(\operatorname{var}(y \mid \omega)\big)}_{\operatorname{E}(\omega)} + \underbrace{\operatorname{var}\!\big(\operatorname{E}(y \mid \omega)\big)}_{\operatorname{var}(\omega)} = \frac{\alpha}{\beta} + \frac{\alpha}{\beta^2} = \frac{\alpha(\beta + 1)}{\beta^2}$$
Matching the Negative Binomial Parametrization

With $p = \dfrac{\beta}{1 + \beta}$ (so that $1 - p = \dfrac{1}{1+\beta}$), the marginal is negative binomial with parameters $(\alpha, p)$, whose standard moments are $\operatorname{E}(y) = \dfrac{\alpha(1-p)}{p}$ and $\operatorname{var}(y) = \dfrac{\alpha(1-p)}{p^2}$. Substituting confirms our result:

$$\frac{\alpha(1-p)}{p} = \alpha \cdot \frac{1/(1+\beta)}{\beta/(1+\beta)} = \frac{\alpha}{\beta}, \qquad \frac{\alpha(1-p)}{p^2} = \frac{\alpha}{\beta} \cdot \frac{1}{p} = \frac{\alpha}{\beta} \cdot \frac{1+\beta}{\beta} = \frac{\alpha(\beta+1)}{\beta^2}.$$

Because $\beta > 0$, the factor $(\beta + 1)/\beta > 1$, so $\operatorname{var}(y) > \operatorname{E}(y)$. The negative binomial is overdispersed relative to the Poisson: mixing over an uncertain rate inflates the variance, which is precisely why the negative binomial is the standard remedy for count data with excess variability.

2. Part B: Moments of the Student-$t$ Posterior

Let $t = \sqrt{n}\,(\mu - \bar{y})/s$, where $s$ is the sample standard deviation (a fixed function of the data). We want the first two posterior moments of $t$. The key modeling choice is what to condition on: conditioning on the scale $\sigma^2$ untangles the two levels of the normal-inverse-$\chi^2$ posterior,

$$\mu \mid \sigma^2, y \sim N\!\left(\bar{y},\; \frac{\sigma^2}{n}\right), \qquad \sigma^2 \mid y \sim \text{Inv-}\chi^2\!\big(n-1,\; s^2\big).$$

Throughout, $n$, $s$, and $\bar{y}$ are treated as constants; the randomness is entirely in $\mu$ and $\sigma^2$.

The First Moment

Conditioning on $\sigma^2$, the inner expectation of $\mu - \bar{y}$ is zero because the conditional mean of $\mu$ is exactly $\bar{y}$. Averaging zero over $\sigma^2$ leaves zero:

$$\operatorname{E}(t) = \operatorname{E}\!\Big( \operatorname{E}\big(t \mid \sigma^2, y\big) \Big) = \operatorname{E}\!\left( \frac{\sqrt{n}}{s}\, \operatorname{E}(\mu - \bar{y} \mid \sigma^2, y) \right) = \operatorname{E}(0) = 0.$$

By symmetry the mean is zero, but for the expectation to actually exist the $t_{n-1}$ density must have a finite first moment, which requires more than one degree of freedom: $n - 1 > 1$, i.e. $n > 2$.

The Second Moment

Applying (2.8), the second term vanishes because the conditional mean $\operatorname{E}(t \mid \sigma^2, y) = 0$ is a constant. Only the expected conditional variance survives:

$$\operatorname{var}(t) = \operatorname{E}\!\Big( \operatorname{var}\big(t \mid \sigma^2, y\big) \Big) + \underbrace{\operatorname{var}\!\Big( \operatorname{E}\big(t \mid \sigma^2, y\big) \Big)}_{=\,\operatorname{var}(0)\,=\,0}.$$

The conditional variance follows from scaling the variance of $\mu$: since $t = \tfrac{\sqrt{n}}{s}(\mu - \bar{y})$ and $\operatorname{var}(\mu \mid \sigma^2, y) = \sigma^2/n$,

$$\operatorname{var}(t \mid \sigma^2, y) = \frac{n}{s^2}\, \operatorname{var}(\mu \mid \sigma^2, y) = \frac{n}{s^2}\cdot\frac{\sigma^2}{n} = \frac{\sigma^2}{s^2}.$$

It remains to average $\sigma^2$ over its scaled inverse-$\chi^2$ posterior. For $\sigma^2 \mid y \sim \text{Inv-}\chi^2(n-1, s^2)$, the mean is $\operatorname{E}(\sigma^2 \mid y) = \dfrac{(n-1)s^2}{(n-1) - 2} = \dfrac{(n-1)s^2}{n-3}$. Therefore:

$$\operatorname{var}(t) = \operatorname{E}\!\left( \frac{\sigma^2}{s^2} \,\middle|\, y \right) = \frac{1}{s^2}\cdot\frac{(n-1)s^2}{n-3} = \frac{n-1}{n-3}.$$
Conclusion for Part B

The posterior of $\sqrt{n}(\mu - \bar{y})/s$ has mean $0$ (requiring $n > 2$) and variance $\dfrac{n-1}{n-3}$ (requiring $n > 3$). This matches the textbook variance of a $t_\nu$ distribution, $\dfrac{\nu}{\nu - 2}$, with $\nu = n - 1$. As $n \to \infty$ the variance tends to $1$ and the distribution approaches the standard normal, recovering the familiar large-sample result that the scale uncertainty washes out.

3. The Common Thread

Both parts are instances of the same continuous-mixture logic. A parameter is integrated out—the Poisson rate $\omega$ in Part A, the scale $\sigma^2$ in Part B—and formulas (2.7) and (2.8) let us read off the marginal moments without ever writing down the full marginal density. In each case the total variance decomposition exposes the source of the extra dispersion: the negative binomial inherits variance $\operatorname{var}(\omega)$ from the uncertain rate, and the Student-$t$ inherits its heavy tails from the uncertain scale. Integrating over a conjugate prior does not merely relocate the mean; it widens the predictive spread in a way that iterated expectation and total variance quantify exactly.
