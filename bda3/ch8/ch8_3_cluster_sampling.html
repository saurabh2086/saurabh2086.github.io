Cluster Sampling and the Black Hole of Unsampled Batches

Why two-stage sampling creates massive population blind spots, the causal trap of cluster sizes, and how Bayesian hierarchical imputation reconstructs the unseen city.

Saurabh Kumar Pandey • Gelman et al., BDA3 §8.3 (pp. 210–212)
Reading Time: ~15 mins • Interactive Decompression

The Logistical Wall and the Core Distinction

Textbook probability assumes an ideal world: draw $n$ names at random from an urn containing all $N$ citizens, knock on their doors, and record their answers.

In reality, three practical barriers shatter this ideal:

  • No Master Roster: A unified list of every individual student or patient rarely exists; registries typically only track schools or hospitals.
  • Geographic Dispersion: Visiting individuals scattered across thousands of square miles exhausts survey budgets on travel alone.
  • Institutional Gates: Surveying students or patients requires negotiating institutional access with specific schools, clinics, or wards first.

To survive these constraints, pollsters sample in stages: first selecting a small handful of hubs—clusters—and then sampling individuals nested inside them.

Stratified Sampling Precision Booster

Every Group Visited

You divide the population into known strata and sample from every single one.

  • ✓ Unsampled groups: Exactly 0
  • ✓ Inferential reality: Direct data from every corner
Cluster Sampling Logistical Compromise

Most Groups Skipped

You visit only a tiny fraction of hubs (e.g., 5 of 100 schools) and interview many individuals inside them.

  • ⚠ Unsampled groups: 95 out of 100 (Black holes!)
  • ⚠ Inferential reality: Zero data for most communities

The Structural Takeaway: In stratified sampling, there are zero unobserved groups. In cluster sampling, budget constraints force you to skip the vast majority of groups. You are left holding a dataset with massive, systemic black holes across the population.

The Origin: The Melbourne Schoolchildren Study

When textbooks present cluster sampling, they often resort to synthetic examples with neat, round numbers. But the example in BDA3 (Section 8.3, p. 211) comes directly from the authors' own field experience in pediatric public health.

In the mid-1990s, John B. Carlin—the second author of BDA3 and a leading Australian biostatistician at the Royal Children's Hospital and Murdoch Children's Research Institute in Melbourne—joined forces with pediatric epidemiologists and co-author Andrew Gelman to investigate a critical public health question:

"What proportion of primary school children in the Melbourne metropolitan area actively walk to school, and how much traffic hazard are they exposed to on a typical morning?"

Published as: Carlin, Stevenson, Roberts, Bennett, Gelman, & Nolan (1997). Walking to school and traffic exposure in Australian children. Australian and New Zealand Journal of Public Health, 21(3), 286–292.

The Real-World Logistical Challenge

Child pedestrian injuries were one of the leading causes of preventable trauma in Australian children. City planners and health officials needed an accurate baseline of how many kids walked, but gathering this data across greater Melbourne was a logistical nightmare:

  • Greater Melbourne is a sprawling metropolitan basin spanning thousands of square kilometers.
  • There were roughly $1{,}000$ primary schools and tens of thousands of young students.
  • Because of decentralized school boards and child privacy safeguards, there was no centralized registry of individual children.

However, the state education department did possess an administrative directory listing every primary school, along with the total number of classrooms in each.

To balance their travel budget with rigorous sampling, Carlin, Gelman, and the research team implemented a two-stage cluster sample:

  1. Stage 1 (Schools): They sampled $J = 72$ primary schools across metropolitan Melbourne.
  2. Stage 2 (Classrooms): Inside each of the 72 chosen schools, they randomly selected 2 classrooms and administered surveys to all students in those rooms.

The 3-Tier Nested Hierarchy

This physical design introduces three distinct, nested tiers of variation:

$$\text{Students } (i) \quad \subset \quad \text{Classrooms } (j) \quad \subset \quad \text{Schools } (k)$$

To track the data and the population, Gelman and Carlin established the following mathematical notation:

Cluster Survey Notation
Symbol Physical Meaning Melbourne Reality
$k$ School index ($k = 1, \dots, K$) $\approx 1{,}000$ schools total in the city; only 72 visited
$M_k$ Number of classes in school $k$ Varies widely (small schools $\approx 4$; large schools $\ge 30$)
$j$ Classroom index ($j = 1, \dots, M_k$) Exactly 2 classes randomly picked per visited school
$N_{jk}$ Number of students in class $j$, school $k$ Class roster size (typically 20–30 children)
$y_{ijk}$ Binary response: $1$ if child walks, $0$ otherwise Observed answer for student $i$

The total population of schoolchildren across all $K$ schools in Melbourne is:

$$N = \sum_{k=1}^K \sum_{j=1}^{M_k} N_{jk}$$

And the ultimate prize—the exact citywide proportion of students who walk to school—is the finite-population mean:

$$\bar{Y} = \frac{1}{N} \sum_{k=1}^K \sum_{j=1}^{M_k} \sum_{i=1}^{N_{jk}} y_{ijk}$$

Notice the sheer magnitude of the unobserved territory: out of roughly $1{,}000$ schools in the city, **over $900$ schools were never visited**. And within the 72 schools that were visited, dozens of classrooms were left unpolled.

How the survey team actually picked those 72 schools turns out to be one of the cleverest tricks in classical survey statistics—and sets the stage for a subtle causal trap.

Probability Proportional to Size and the "Self-Weighting" Illusion

Once the researchers decided to sample 72 schools, they faced an immediate mathematical dilemma: schools in Melbourne are not the same size.

A small community school in an outer green wedge might have only $M_k = 4$ classrooms, while a large suburban primary school might boast $M_k = 30$ classrooms.

Suppose you took the naive route and sampled schools with equal probability (giving each school a flat $1/1{,}000$ chance of selection). What happens to the students inside?

  • A child in the small school has a $2 / 4 = 50\%$ chance of having their classroom picked.
  • A child in the large school has only a $2 / 30 \approx 6.7\%$ chance of having their classroom picked.

An equal-probability school draw violently over-represents children from small schools, giving a rural child nearly eight times the voice of a suburban child!

The Classical Ingenuity: Probability Proportional to Size (PPS)

To fix this imbalance, survey statisticians in the mid-twentieth century developed an elegant sampling scheme called Probability Proportional to Size (PPS).

Instead of giving each school an equal lottery ticket, the survey administrators make the probability of picking school $k$ strictly proportional to its number of classrooms, $M_k$:

$$\Pr(\text{School } k \text{ is sampled}) = \frac{J \cdot M_k}{M_{\text{total}}}$$

where $J = 72$ is the number of sampled schools, and $M_{\text{total}} = \sum_{k=1}^K M_k$ is the total count of classrooms across all Melbourne.

Under PPS, a large school with 30 classes is ten times more likely to be chosen by the survey team than a small school with 3 classes.

The Algebraic Cancellation: The Equal-Probability "Miracle"

Now consider what happens in Stage 2. Once the surveyors arrive at school $k$, they randomly pick exactly 2 classrooms from the $M_k$ classrooms in that building:

$$\Pr(\text{Classroom } j \text{ sampled} \mid \text{School } k \text{ sampled}) = \frac{2}{M_k}$$

By the basic multiplication rule of probability, what is the net, overall chance that any specific classroom in Melbourne lands in the survey?

$$\begin{aligned} \Pr(\text{Classroom } j \text{ in School } k \text{ is in sample}) &= \Pr(\text{School } k \text{ sampled}) \times \Pr(\text{Class } j \text{ sampled} \mid \text{School } k) \\[8pt] &= \left( \frac{J \cdot \mathbf{M_k}}{M_{\text{total}}} \right) \times \left( \frac{2}{\mathbf{M_k}} \right) \\[8pt] &= \frac{2J}{M_{\text{total}}} = \mathbf{\text{Constant!}} \end{aligned}$$

The Mathematical Cancellation: Notice that $M_k$ appears in the numerator of the school selection probability, and in the denominator of the classroom selection probability. They cancel out completely!

Assuming classrooms across the city are roughly comparable in size ($N_{jk} \approx \bar{N}$), every single child in the entire metropolitan area of Melbourne has the exact same probability of being included in the survey.

The Classical Survey Triumph

In classical sampling theory, a design where every unit has an equal inclusion probability is called self-weighting.

This is celebrated in survey textbooks as the holy grail:

  • You do not need to calculate or apply individual survey weights ($\frac{1}{\pi_i}$).
  • The raw, unweighted arithmetic sample average of your data: $$\bar{y}_{\text{sample}} = \frac{1}{n} \sum_{i=1}^n y_i$$ is mathematically guaranteed to be an unbiased estimator of the true population proportion $\bar{Y}$.

It seems like the survey team has achieved total victory. The logistical problem is solved (they only visited 72 schools), and the statistical problem is solved (the naive sample mean is unbiased).

The Lingering Puzzle: If the classical sample average is guaranteed to be unbiased across hypothetical repeated samples, why does Gelman write on page 211 that Bayesian analysis is essential? How do we properly model the nested structure of classrooms within schools, and what does it take to reconstruct the entire unseen city?

The Hierarchical Architecture: Connecting Classrooms, Schools, and the Unseen City

Let us step into the shoes of the survey statistician staring at their collected data sheets.

We have surveyed children from 72 schools. But the moment we look inside any single sampled school, an uncomfortable reality emerges:

The Within-School Missingness: School $k$ has $M_k = 20$ classrooms, but our survey team only walked into 2 of them. We have zero direct data for the other 18 classrooms in that same building!

How do we estimate the walking rate of those 18 unobserved classrooms?

We cannot treat them as complete strangers. Classrooms inside the same school are physically and socially connected: they share the same school location, the same neighborhood traffic, the same school crossing guards, and the same local catchment boundaries. Yet, they are not clones: a classroom of fifth-graders might walk independently, while a classroom of first-graders might be driven by parents.

Level 1: The Classroom Likelihood and Within-School Hierarchy

For each surveyed classroom $j$ in school $k$, let $\bar{y}_{.jk}$ denote the observed proportion of children who walk to school. We model this observed classroom average as a noisy observation of a true underlying classroom walking probability, $\theta_{jk}$:

$$\bar{y}_{.jk} \mid \theta_{jk} \sim N\left(\theta_{jk}, \; \sigma_{jk}^2\right), \quad \text{where } \sigma_{jk}^2 = \frac{\bar{y}_{.jk}(1 - \bar{y}_{.jk})}{N_{jk}} \quad (8.9)$$

*(Here $N_{jk}$ is the number of students in that class. Gelman uses a normal approximation to the binomial to make the hierarchical algebra transparent).*

Now we introduce the first tier of our hierarchy: connecting the classrooms within a school.

Because all classes in school $k$ share the school's local environment, their individual latent rates $\theta_{jk}$ fluctuate around a shared school-level mean, $\theta_k$:

$$\theta_{jk} \sim N\left(\theta_k, \; \tau_{\text{class}}^2\right) \quad (8.10\text{a})$$

Here, $\tau_{\text{class}}^2$ captures grade-to-grade, classroom-level variance. Through this distribution, the 2 surveyed classrooms allow us to estimate the school parameter $\theta_k$—which in turn allows us to infer the walking behavior of the 18 unsurveyed classrooms in that building!

Level 2: The Missing Schools Across Melbourne

Now zoom out to the entire metropolitan area.

We visited 72 schools, but Melbourne has roughly $1{,}000$ primary schools. More than 900 schools in the city have zero surveyed classrooms, zero student responses, and zero direct data rows.

Just as classrooms within a school share a common school identity, schools across Melbourne share a broader municipal context. They must be linked through a school-level hierarchy.

Our first instinct might be to write down a simple exchangeable normal model across all schools:

$$\theta_k \sim N(\mu, \; \tau_{\text{school}}^2) \quad \text{[Naive / Non-ignorable]}$$

Why is this naive formulation deeply flawed? This brings us directly to the causal mechanism of the survey.

The Causal Justification: Why School Size ($M_k$) Must Be in the Model

Remember how the 72 schools were chosen: the researchers used Probability Proportional to Size (PPS). A school with 30 classes had ten times the chance of selection compared to a school with 3 classes.

We can see the causal danger immediately by drawing the Causal Directed Acyclic Graph (DAG):

Causal DAG: School Size as a Confounder

Figure: The sampling mechanism creates a classic common-cause fork ($I_k \leftarrow M_k \rightarrow \theta_k$).

Look at the paths in the diagram:

  • Design Path ($M_k \longrightarrow I_k$): Large schools are sampled far more frequently than small schools.
  • Environmental Path ($M_k \longrightarrow \theta_k$): In urban geography, large primary schools are concentrated in dense inner-suburban corridors with sidewalks and short walking distances. Small schools are often in low-density, sprawling outer fringes where distances are large and children are driven.
  • Conditioning on Data ($I_k = 1$): Because our dataset only contains the 72 schools that were actually sampled, our analysis is implicitly conditioned on $I_k = 1$.

If we omit school size $M_k$ from the model, $M_k$ acts as an unblocked confounder. Our 72 sampled schools are disproportionately large, and therefore have systematically higher walking rates. A naive flat model $\theta_k \sim N(\mu, \tau_{\text{school}}^2)$ would mistakenly conclude that all $1{,}000$ schools in Melbourne walk at high inner-city rates!

This is why Gelman states the foundational principle of Bayesian survey analysis:

"We include the number of classes $M_k$ in the school-level model because of the principle that all information used in the design should be included in the analysis. In this survey, the sampling probabilities depend on the school sizes $M_k$, and so the design is ignorable only for a model that includes the $M_k$’s."

— Andrew Gelman et al., BDA3 Section 8.3, p. 211

By making the school-level mean a function of $M_k$:

$$\theta_k \mid M_k \sim N\left(\alpha + \beta M_k, \; \tau_{\text{school}}^2\right) \quad (8.10\text{b})$$

we block the confounding backdoor path. Once we know $M_k$, knowing whether a school happened to be sampled conveys zero additional information about its walking rate:

$$p(\theta_k \mid M_k, I_k = 1) = p(\theta_k \mid M_k)$$

The sampling design becomes ignorable. The regression slope $\beta$ captures the relationship between school size and walking, allowing us to generate realistic walking rates for the unsampled schools using their actual administrative sizes $M_k$.

The Complete Formulation for the Analysis

Putting all three tiers together gives the complete hierarchical generative model for the Melbourne survey:

The Complete 3-Tier Bayesian Model (BDA3 Eqs. 8.9 & 8.10)
Level 1: Observed Classroom Likelihood
$$\bar{y}_{.jk} \mid \theta_{jk} \sim N\left(\theta_{jk}, \; \frac{\bar{y}_{.jk}(1 - \bar{y}_{.jk})}{N_{jk}}\right)$$

Observed sample proportions in surveyed classes $j \in \{1, 2\}$ within sampled schools $k \in \{1, \dots, 72\}$.

Level 2: Within-School Classroom Hierarchy
$$\theta_{jk} \mid \theta_k \sim N\left(\theta_k, \; \tau_{\text{class}}^2\right)$$

Connects surveyed and unsurveyed classrooms within school $k$, with classroom-level variance $\tau_{\text{class}}^2$.

Level 3: Citywide School-Level Regression (Design-Ignorable)
$$\theta_k \mid M_k \sim N\left(\alpha + \beta M_k, \; \tau_{\text{school}}^2\right)$$

Connects sampled and unsampled schools across Melbourne, conditioned on school size $M_k$, with school variance $\tau_{\text{school}}^2$.

Hyperparameter Priors
$$p(\alpha, \beta, \tau_{\text{class}}, \tau_{\text{school}}) \propto 1 \quad \text{(or weakly informative half-$t$ on variances)}$$

Noninformative or regularizing priors on the superpopulation regression coefficients and variance components.

With the complete generative model assembled, we now have a mathematically sound bridge between the 72 visited schools and the hundreds of unseen schools across Melbourne.

The next question is: how do we use this model to simulate and reconstruct the entire city's population mean?

The 4-Step Bayesian Reconstruction Engine

Having written down the 3-tier hierarchical model, how do we use it to calculate the actual municipal walking percentage across Melbourne?

In classical survey statistics, analysts often stop at estimating superpopulation parameters. But Bayesian survey analysis is resolutely focused on the finite population: there are real children walking to real schools on Tuesday morning. Our goal is to obtain the posterior distribution of the finite-population mean:

$$\bar{Y} = \frac{1}{N} \sum_{k=1}^K N_k \bar{y}_{..k}$$

Gelman et al. (BDA3 p. 211–212) outline an exact 4-step simulation recipe that turns MCMC draws into finite-population predictions:

Step 1

Fit the Model to Sampled Clusters

We fit the 3-tier model to the observed data from the 72 schools and their 144 surveyed classrooms using MCMC (e.g., Stan or PyMC). From this fit, we obtain $S$ posterior simulation draws (e.g., $S = 2{,}000$) of:

  • The global hyperparameters: $(\alpha, \beta, \tau_{\text{class}}, \tau_{\text{school}})$
  • The school-level parameters for the 72 visited schools: $\theta_k^{\text{sampled}}$
  • The classroom-level parameters for the 144 visited classrooms: $\theta_{jk}^{\text{sampled}}$
Step 2

Simulate Latent Parameters for Unsampled Schools and Classes

For each posterior draw $s \in \{1, \dots, S\}$, we simulate the unobserved world from the hierarchical prior:

$$\begin{aligned} \text{For each unseen school } k \in \text{Unsampled}: &\quad \theta_k^{(s)} \sim N\left(\alpha^{(s)} + \beta^{(s)} M_k, \; (\tau_{\text{school}}^{(s)})^2\right) \\[6pt] \text{For each unseen classroom } j \text{ in school } k: &\quad \theta_{jk}^{(s)} \sim N\left(\theta_k^{(s)}, \; (\tau_{\text{class}}^{(s)})^2\right) \end{aligned}$$

Notice that we plug in the known administrative size $M_k$ of each unsampled school. This ensures that the generated rates reflect the correct urban-to-rural distribution across the city.

Step 3

Predict the School-Level Averages ($\bar{y}_{..k}$)

For every school in Melbourne, we now compute or simulate the true average response of its students: $$\bar{y}_{..k} = \frac{1}{N_k} \sum_{j=1}^{M_k} N_{jk} \bar{y}_{.jk}$$

  • For Sampled Schools: $\bar{y}_{..k}$ is an average over the 2 classrooms we actually observed (where $\bar{y}_{.jk}$ is fixed observed data) and the $M_k - 2$ classrooms we simulated. This is the exact finite-population blend: $$\bar{y}_{..k} = \frac{N_{\text{obs}} \bar{y}_{\text{obs}, k} + N_{\text{mis}} \bar{y}_{\text{mis}, k}}{N_k}$$
  • For Unsampled Schools: Every single classroom is missing. Gelman shows we can integrate out the intermediate classroom parameters and simulate the entire school average in a single step:
$$\bar{y}_{..k} \sim N\left(\alpha + \beta M_k, \; \mathbf{\tau_{\text{school}}^2 + \frac{\tau_{\text{class}}^2}{M_k} + \frac{\sigma^2}{N_k}}\right)$$

Disassembling the 3-Tier Variance Formula

Look at how each tier of uncertainty contributes to our prediction of an unseen school:

  1. $\tau_{\text{school}}^2$ (Irreducible School Variance): How much schools naturally deviate from the regression line $\alpha + \beta M_k$. This term does not shrink with school size.
  2. $\frac{\tau_{\text{class}}^2}{M_k}$ (Averaged Classroom Variance): Classroom-to-classroom shocks cancel each other out as we average across all $M_k$ classrooms in the building.
  3. $\frac{\sigma^2}{N_k}$ (Averaged Student Sampling Noise): Individual binomial randomness ($0$ or $1$) washes away as the total student enrollment $N_k$ grows large.
Step 4

Combine to Compute the Population Mean ($\bar{Y}$)

Finally, for each posterior simulation draw $s$, we take the enrollment-weighted sum across all $K$ schools in the city:

$$\bar{Y}^{(s)} = \frac{1}{N} \sum_{k=1}^K N_k \bar{y}_{..k}^{(s)}$$

Repeating this across all $S = 2{,}000$ draws produces an empirical histogram of $\bar{Y}$. This histogram represents our exact posterior distribution for the proportion of walking children in Melbourne—fully accounting for sample noise, classroom clustering, school heterogeneity, and finite-population boundaries!

The Critical Prerequisite: Notice that in Step 4, we multiply each school average by $N_k$, the total number of students in school $k$. This requires knowing the enrollment $N_k$ for every school in the population from administrative records. (What happens when cluster sizes are unknown? Gelman explores this in the next example on Alcoholics Anonymous groups, where $(N_k, \theta_k)$ must be modeled jointly.)

The Small-Fraction Shortcut: When Unseen Clusters Dominate

The 4-step reconstruction recipe is mathematically complete and exact for any cluster survey. But running thousands of posterior simulations across hundreds of unobserved schools and classes can be computationally heavy.

On page 212 of BDA3, Gelman points out an elegant shortcut that survey practitioners rely on whenever the sampled clusters form a small fraction of the total population:

"For inference about schools in the Melbourne area, the superpopulation quantities $\theta_{jk}$ and $\theta_k$ can be viewed as intermediate quantities, useful for the ultimate goal of estimating the finite-population average $\bar{Y}$... In general practice, however (and including this particular survey), the number of schools sampled is a small fraction of the total in the city, and thus we can approximate the average of any variable in the population by its mean in the superpopulation distribution."

— Gelman et al., BDA3 Section 8.3, p. 212

The Geometry of the Melbourne Numbers ($72$ vs. $1{,}000$)

To see why this approximation works, look at the proportions in the finite-population sum of Step 4:

$$\bar{Y} = \frac{1}{N} \left( \sum_{k \in \text{Sampled}} N_k \bar{y}_{..k} \; + \sum_{k \in \text{Unsampled}} N_k \bar{y}_{..k} \right)$$

In the Australian study:

  • Sampled Schools ($J = 72$): Represent roughly $7\%$ of the city's school population.
  • Unsampled Schools ($K - J \approx 928$): Represent roughly $93\%$ of the city's school population!

Over nine-tenths of the sum comes from schools where we have no direct data rows.

For each of those 928 unseen schools, its simulated average in Step 3 is generated as:

$$\bar{y}_{..k} = \alpha + \beta M_k + \epsilon_k, \quad \text{where } \epsilon_k \sim N\left(0, \; \tau_{\text{school}}^2 + \frac{\tau_{\text{class}}^2}{M_k} + \frac{\sigma^2}{N_k}\right)$$

Notice what happens when you sum over 928 independent random deviations $\epsilon_k$:

$$\sum_{k \in \text{Unsampled}} N_k \epsilon_k \approx 0$$

By the Law of Large Numbers across clusters, the idiosyncratic school-level shocks cancel each other out! One school in Northcote walks a little more than predicted by its size ($\epsilon_k > 0$), while another in Box Hill walks a little less ($\epsilon_k < 0$). In the grand metropolitan total, these independent variations average out to nearly zero.

The Mathematical Collapse into Equation (8.11)

Because the 72 visited schools are such a tiny fraction of the total ($7\%$), and because the random shocks for the remaining 93% wash away, we can replace each school's latent parameter directly with its mathematical expectation under the superpopulation regression:

$$E[\theta_k \mid M_k] = \alpha + \beta M_k$$

Plugging this expectation into the finite-population sum yields BDA3 Equation (8.11):

$$\bar{Y} \approx \frac{1}{N} \sum_{k=1}^K N_k E(\theta_k) = \mathbf{\frac{1}{N} \sum_{k=1}^K N_k (\alpha + \beta M_k)} \quad (8.11)$$

Notice the remarkable algebraic simplicity of this formula. We can factor out the regression coefficients:

$$\bar{Y} \approx \alpha + \beta \cdot \underbrace{\left( \frac{\sum_{k=1}^K N_k M_k}{\sum_{k=1}^K N_k} \right)}_{\bar{M}_{\text{weighted}}}$$

The Practical Computational Payoff: You do not need to simulate thousands of latent classroom and school parameters for 928 unobserved schools during MCMC. For every posterior draw of the hyperparameter pair $(\alpha^{(s)}, \beta^{(s)})$, you simply multiply $\beta^{(s)}$ by $\bar{M}_{\text{weighted}}$—the known, population-weighted average school size in Melbourne from the education registry—and add $\alpha^{(s)}$.

The Counterfactual Catastrophe: When Does This Shortcut Fail?

It is tempting to adopt Equation (8.11) as a universal shortcut for all survey analysis. But it is vital to understand the exact conditions under which this approximation collapses.

Failure Mode: When the Sampled Fraction is Large

Suppose the survey budget was enormous, and researchers visited 950 out of 1,000 schools (a 95% sample):

  1. Throwing Away Observed Truth: For 950 schools, you actually know their real classroom data! If an inner-city school had 95% walkers due to a localized transit initiative, but the regression line $\alpha + \beta M_k$ predicted 50%, Equation (8.11) throws away the observed reality and forces the school back onto the line.
  2. The Illusion of Uncertainty: If you survey 100% of schools and 100% of children, your uncertainty about the finite population $\bar{Y}$ must be identically zero (you counted every single living child!). But Equation (8.11) still carries posterior uncertainty about $(\alpha, \beta)$, falsely claiming that the citywide total is uncertain!

Equation (8.11) is valid only when the sampling fraction $J / K$ is near zero, where unsampled clusters overwhelmingly dominate the finite population sum and the superpopulation expectation becomes an accurate proxy for the census total.
