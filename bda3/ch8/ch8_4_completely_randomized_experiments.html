Designed Experiments: Completely Randomized Experiments

How to think about an experiment from duplicate parallel universes to the real world—why we need the stability assumption, how randomization makes treatment assignment ignorable, and why within-person correlation disappears from the likelihood.

Saurabh Kumar Pandey • Gelman et al., BDA3 §8.4
Reading Time: ~12 mins • Study Notes

1. Starting from the Simplest Ideal: Duplicate Universes and the 3D System

In a sample survey, we passively observe a world that already exists. In an experiment, we actively intervene: we assign treatments to units, and we want to know what effect that treatment actually had.

Before looking at any statistical formulas, let's picture the simplest possible setup where measuring a treatment effect would be effortless.

One Person in Two Duplicate Universes

Suppose we have just one person (Person 1) in a single universe, and we give them the basic treatment $A$, observing their outcome $y_{A1}$.

Now imagine we can create an exact duplicate universe—containing the exact same Person 1 at the exact same moment—and in that duplicate universe we give them the new treatment $B$, observing $y_{B1}$.

$$\text{Treatment Effect for Person 1} = y_{A1} - y_{B1}$$

Because the person is identical in both universes, we don't have to worry about any confounding differences. And we can imagine doing the exact same thing for a second person (Person 2), giving them Treatment $A$ in one universe ($y_{A2}$) and Treatment $B$ in their duplicate universe ($y_{B2}$).

A Three-Dimensional System of Universes $(X, Y, Z)$

Now, what if the biological or physical response of a person isn't a rigid, fixed number, but has some natural randomness to it? If we replicate the system for the exact same person and treatment multiple times, we can arrange all these parallel universes along a three-dimensional grid:

  • $X$-Axis (Treatments): Moving from Treatment $A$ to Treatment $B$.
  • $Y$-Axis (System Copies / Replications): Repeated copies of the exact same person-and-treatment system, capturing how the outcome fluctuates across repeated runs.
  • $Z$-Axis (People): Moving from Person 1 to Person 2, Person 3, $\dots$, Person $n$.

Collapsing the $Y$-Axis by Taking the Expectation

Look along the $Y$-axis (the system copies) for Person 1 in the grid above. Even for the exact same person, $y_{A1}$ and $y_{B1}$ can take slightly different values from one copy of the system to the next. That means any single realization mixes together two things:

  1. The systematic shift from Treatment $A$ to Treatment $B$ (along the $X$-axis).
  2. The random copy-to-copy fluctuation of the system (along the $Y$-axis).

How do we separate the treatment effect from that $Y$-axis noise? We take the expected value across the system copies, collapsing the $Y$-axis down to $\text{E}(y_{Ai}) - \text{E}(y_{Bi})$.

2. Breaking Symmetry and Why We Need the Stability Assumption

So far, our parallel universes have been completely isolated and symmetric—every person lived in their own private bubble with no external environment and no interaction with anyone else. Now let's walk this idealized setup back toward the real world step by step.

Step 1: Adding the Environment and Breaking Clone Symmetry

First, people don't exist in a vacuum—they live inside an environment, and they each carry their own personal context (age, baseline health, genetics, habits).

When we move along the $Z$-axis from Person 1 to Person $n$, we break the symmetry between people: each individual $i$ now has a different personal context ($\text{Context}_1 \neq \text{Context}_2 \neq \dots \neq \text{Context}_n$). So for any person $i$, their probabilistic outcome is shaped by three things at once:

  • The Treatment ($A$, $B$, or a third state where the person receives no treatment at all), governed by treatment parameters.
  • The Environment, governed by environment parameters.
  • Personal Context, specific to Person $i$.

Step 2: Bringing People Together into Shared Universes (Why the Stability Assumption Appears)

Next, instead of keeping Person 1, Person 2, $\dots$, Person $n$ in separate private universes, we bring all $n$ people together into shared universes: a shared Treatment $A$ universe, a shared Treatment $B$ universe, and a shared No-Treatment universe.

Look at what happens the moment people share the same universe: Person 1 and Person 2 can interact! They can influence each other's personal context, and through that interaction, one person's treatment can indirectly spill over and change another person's outcome.

Where the Stability Assumption Comes From: If Person 1's treatment can alter Person 2's outcome, then Person 2 doesn't just have two potential outcomes $(y_{A2}, y_{B2})$—their outcome depends on what treatment everyone else received! To prevent that explosion, we introduce the stability assumption: the treatment applied to unit $i$ has no effect on the potential outcomes in the other $n - 1$ units.

Once the stability assumption holds, all the potential outcomes for the $n$ people in our study reduce cleanly to an $n \times 2$ matrix of observables $(y_{Ai}, y_{Bi})$ for $i = 1, \dots, n$:

Step 3: Conditioning on $\theta$ and Defining the Overall Causal Estimand

To compare Treatment $A$ and Treatment $B$ fairly without being thrown off by random fluctuations in the environment and personal context, we condition on the underlying parameters $\theta$ governing the complete-data distribution $p(y_{Ai}, y_{Bi} \mid \theta)$.

Depending on whether we care about the broader process generating the data or just the specific $n$ people in the room, we can write the overall causal effect in two ways:

1. Superpopulation Causal Effect

Thinking of our units as drawn from a larger population distribution $p(y_{Ai}, y_{Bi} \mid \theta)$, the average causal effect is a function of the parameters $\theta$:

$$\text{E}(y_{Ai} - y_{Bi} \mid \theta) = \text{E}(y_{Ai} \mid \theta) - \text{E}(y_{Bi} \mid \theta)$$

Because expectation is linear, the expected same-person difference splits into the difference between the two marginal expectations.

2. Finite-Population Causal Effect

If our focus is specifically on the finite group of units under study, the causal effect is simply the average difference across those units:

$$\bar{y}_A - \bar{y}_B = \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^n y_{Ai} - \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^n y_{Bi}$$

Here, half of the $y_{Ai}$ values and half of the $y_{Bi}$ values are missing and must be imputed from the posterior predictive distribution.

3. The Person in the Middle and Collapsing to Reality ($I_i$ and $p(I)$)

Suppose Treatment $A$ is governed by a parameter $\theta_A$ and Treatment $B$ is governed by a separate parameter $\theta_B$. Even if $\theta_A$ and $\theta_B$ are completely independent of one another, look at the causal structure for any given person $i$:

Both $y_{Ai}$ and $y_{Bi}$ happen to the same person $i$. If Person $i$ has a strong constitution or mild symptoms, both $y_{Ai}$ and $y_{Bi}$ will reflect that. In other words, the person sits right in the middle as a common cause of both potential outcomes:

Figure 2: Within-Person Coupling Across Rows — Why $y_{Ai}$ and $y_{Bi}$ Are Correlated Given $\theta$

The Inclusion Indicator $I_i = (I_{Ai}, I_{Bi})$

When we run the actual experiment in our single real universe, a person who gets Treatment $A$ cannot also get Treatment $B$. As shown in Figure 2, one side of every row becomes missing (`?`).

To keep track of which universe we actually observe for each person $i$, we define a two-component indicator $I_i = (I_{Ai}, I_{Bi})$:

  • $I_i = (1, 0)$: Unit $i$ receives Treatment $A$ (we observe $y_{Ai}$, while $y_{Bi}$ is missing).
  • $I_i = (0, 1)$: Unit $i$ receives Treatment $B$ (we observe $y_{Bi}$, while $y_{Ai}$ is missing).
  • $I_i = (0, 0)$: Unit $i$ receives neither treatment (for example, a unit whose treatment has not yet been applied; both $y_{Ai}$ and $y_{Bi}$ are missing).

Because no unit can receive both treatments at once, $I_{Ai}$ and $I_{Bi}$ can never both equal $1$. (And when $I_i = (0, 0)$ cannot occur, $I_{Bi}$ is simply $1 - I_{Ai}$.)

The Probability of Assignment $p(I)$ in a Completely Randomized Experiment

Suppose $n$ is even, and we randomly assign exactly half of the units ($\frac{n}{2}$) to Treatment $A$ and the other half ($\frac{n}{2}$) to Treatment $B$.

Out of $n$ units, there are $\binom{n}{n/2}$ possible ways to choose which $\frac{n}{2}$ units receive Treatment $A$. Because complete randomization gives every valid split the exact same chance:

$$p(I \mid y, \phi) = p(I) = \begin{cases} \binom{n}{n/2}^{-1} & \text{if } \sum_{i=1}^n I_{Ai} = \sum_{i=1}^n I_{Bi} = \frac{n}{2}, \;\; I_{Ai} \neq I_{Bi} \text{ for all } i, \\[8pt] 0 & \text{otherwise.} \end{cases}$$

Notice why $p(I \mid y, \phi) = p(I)$: the probability of any valid assignment is just the known number $\binom{n}{n/2}^{-1}$, with no dependence on the potential outcomes $y$ or any unknown parameters $\phi$. That makes the treatment assignment known and strongly ignorable.

4. What We Can Learn vs. What Vanishes in the Likelihood

Because the treatment assignment $p(I)$ is ignorable, we can infer the parameters $\theta$ directly from the observed data using $p(\theta \mid y_{\text{obs}}) \propto p(\theta)p(y_{\text{obs}} \mid \theta)$.

How $p(y_{\text{obs}} \mid \theta)$ Splits into Two Separate Products

Look back at Figure 2: for every person who received Treatment $A$, $y_{Bi}$ is missing (`?`), so we integrate $p(y_{Ai}, y_{Bi} \mid \theta)$ over $y_{Bi}$, which leaves just the marginal distribution $p(y_{Ai} \mid \theta)$. Similarly, for every person who received Treatment $B$, integrating over their missing $y_{Ai}$ leaves $p(y_{Bi} \mid \theta)$.

Multiplying across all $n$ units gives the observed-data likelihood:

$$p(y_{\text{obs}} \mid \theta) = \prod_{i:\, I_i = (1,0)} p(y_{Ai} \mid \theta) \prod_{i:\, I_i = (0,1)} p(y_{Bi} \mid \theta)$$

Look at how clean that is: the first product only involves the parameters $\theta_A$ governing the marginal distribution of $y_{Ai}$, and the second product only involves the parameters $\theta_B$ governing the marginal distribution of $y_{Bi}$. If $\theta_A$ and $\theta_B$ are independent in the prior, we can analyze the Treatment $A$ group and the Treatment $B$ group completely separately!

Why Finite-Population Inference Is Trickier Here Than in a Survey

What if we want the finite-population causal effect $\bar{y}_A - \bar{y}_B$ for our $n$ units? Once we have posterior draws of $\theta$, we need to fill in the missing half of the $n \times 2$ table—$(y_{A\,\text{mis}}, y_{B\,\text{mis}})$—by drawing from the posterior predictive distribution:

$$p(y_{\text{mis}} \mid \theta, y_{\text{obs}}) = \prod_{i:\, I_i = (1,0)} p(y_{Bi} \mid \theta, y_{Ai}) \prod_{i:\, I_i = (0,1)} p(y_{Ai} \mid \theta, y_{Bi})$$

And here is the catch that we saw in Figure 2:

  • To predict Person 1's missing $y_{B1}$ given their observed $y_{A1}$—that is, $p(y_{Bi} \mid \theta, y_{Ai})$—we need the full joint distribution $p(y_{Ai}, y_{Bi} \mid \theta)$, including the within-person correlation parameter $\theta_{AB} = \text{corr}(y_{Ai}, y_{Bi} \mid \theta)$.
  • But $\theta_{AB}$ never appears in the likelihood $p(y_{\text{obs}} \mid \theta)$! Because we never observe both $y_{Ai}$ and $y_{Bi}$ on the same person, the data gives us zero direct information about $\theta_{AB}$. Its posterior is just its conditional prior $p(\theta_{AB} \mid \theta_A, \theta_B)$ averaged over the posterior of $(\theta_A, \theta_B)$.

5. When the Unknown Correlation Stops Mattering: Large-Sample Correspondence

If the data tells us nothing about the within-person correlation $\text{corr}(y_{Ai}, y_{Bi} \mid \theta)$, does that mean our causal conclusions are always at the mercy of an untestable prior guess about $\theta_{AB}$?

No—for large populations, that sensitivity vanishes completely.

Suppose our $n$ experimental units were themselves randomly sampled from a much larger finite population of $N$ units, and we want to estimate the average treatment effect $\bar{y}_A - \bar{y}_B$ across all $N$ units in that population (or the superpopulation difference $\text{E}(y_{Ai} \mid \theta_A) - \text{E}(y_{Bi} \mid \theta_B)$).

When both $n$ and $N / n$ are large, the vast majority of the $N$ people in the population received neither treatment in our experiment—so their missing outcomes depend only on the separate marginal parameters $\theta_A$ and $\theta_B$, not on the within-person conditioning $p(y_{Bi} \mid \theta, y_{Ai})$. By the Central Limit Theorem, the posterior distribution of the causal effect simplifies to:

$$(\bar{y}_A - \bar{y}_B) \mid y_{\text{obs}} \;\approx\; \mathcal{N}\!\left(\bar{y}_{A\,\text{obs}} - \bar{y}_{B\,\text{obs}}, \;\; \frac{2}{n}\left(s_{A\,\text{obs}}^2 + s_{B\,\text{obs}}^2\right)\right)$$

where $\bar{y}_{A\,\text{obs}}, \bar{y}_{B\,\text{obs}}$ and $s_{A\,\text{obs}}^2, s_{B\,\text{obs}}^2$ are the sample means and sample variances of the observed outcomes in the two treatment groups (each of size $\frac{n}{2}$, so $\frac{s_{A\,\text{obs}}^2}{n/2} + \frac{s_{B\,\text{obs}}^2}{n/2} = \frac{2}{n}(s_{A\,\text{obs}}^2 + s_{B\,\text{obs}}^2)$).
