Simple Random Sampling of a Finite Population

How estimating weekly food expenditure across a finite community becomes a clean game of predicting un-surveyed households, and how Bayesian first principles naturally derive the classical Finite Population Correction without arbitrary bookkeeping tricks.

Saurabh Kumar Pandey • Gelman et al., BDA3 §8.3 (p. 205–216) • October 2026
14 min read Interactive Policy Lab ↓
Executive Decompression Brief

Classical survey statistics presents the Finite Population Correction $(1 - n/N)$ as an algebraic postulate applied to confidence intervals. In Bayesian data analysis, however, finite-population inference is re-anchored as a pure prediction problem: given observed food diaries $y_{\text{obs}}$ from $n = 400$ residents, we predict the missing balance $y_{\text{mis}}$ for the remaining $N - n = 19{,}600$ citizens. Once the missing ledger entries are imputed, non-linear municipal decisions—such as food poverty headcount, emergency nutrition voucher reserves, and solvency stress thresholds—are evaluated without Taylor-series approximations.

01 The Problem: Surveying Food Expenditure in a Finite Community

Consider a municipal district with a finite population of exactly $N = 20{,}000$ residents (or households). Ahead of an annual public health and social welfare review, city officials must estimate the population-wide average weekly food expenditure: $y_1, y_2, \dots, y_N$, where $y_i$ is the amount spent on food by the $i$th person (Gelman et al., BDA3 p. 205).

The target estimand is the exact population mean:

$$\bar{y} = \frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^N y_i = \frac{y_1 + y_2 + \dots + y_N}{N}$$

Conducting an exhaustive, in-person nutritional and grocery audit of all 20,000 households—collecting itemized grocery receipts, dietary logs, and market bills—would exhaust the city's entire annual public health budget. Instead, the department surveys a simple random sample of $n = 400$ residents who record their detailed weekly food spending.

What does a Bayesian model assume about this finite community before the first survey diary is returned?

The Foundational Assumption: Exchangeability

Before inspecting individual grocery receipts or demographic forms, the city treats the 20,000 residents as exchangeable.

The Philosophical Meaning: Imagine an administrator walked into the municipal registry office and randomly permuted the identification numbers of all 20,000 residents. Your prior belief about the community's overall food expenditure would not change by a single cent. The citizen IDs, housing unit numbers, and filing order carry zero inherent information about grocery habits. Without inspecting specific household covariates (such as income or family size), you have no prior basis to believe Resident #14 spends any more on food than Resident #18,721.

Exchangeability Is Not Independence: It is vital to recognize that these residents are not independent. If the first 50 surveyed households report shockingly high food bills driven by unexpected supermarket grocery inflation, you immediately update your belief that the remaining 19,950 un-surveyed residents are also spending more on food. The residents are tied together through a shared local food market, regional grocery supply chains, and prevailing economic conditions. Exchangeability simply expresses complete symmetry in our initial state of knowledge: before the survey begins, every resident stands on identical epistemic footing.

Translating Symmetry into Mathematics: The Mixture Integral (Page 205)

We assume that there is a common economic and market context represented by parameters $\theta$ (such as prevailing grocery inflation, local food retail prices, and general community wage levels) that drives these food expenditures.

Graphical Model: Conditional Independence Given $\theta$
Key Insight: There are no direct horizontal arrows connecting $y_i$ to $y_j$. Once the common state $\theta$ is fixed, knowing the weekly food spending of Person 1 tells you nothing new about Person 2. The outcomes are conditionally independent given $\theta$: $$p(y_1, y_2, \dots, y_N \mid \theta) = \prod_{i=1}^N p(y_i \mid \theta)$$

Now, write the joint probability distribution of the entire population food expenditure vector $y$:

$$p(y) = p(y_1, y_2, y_3, \dots, y_N)$$

Because we do not know the true economic and market context $\theta$ with certainty, the Law of Total Probability tells us to average over all possible values of $\theta$, weighted by our prior beliefs $p(\theta)$:

$$p(y) = \int p(y_1, y_2, y_3, \dots, y_N \mid \theta) \, p(\theta) \, d\theta$$

Finally, substituting the conditional independence from our diagram above into this integral gives Gelman's exact exchangeability formula:

$$p(y) = \int \left( \prod_{i=1}^N p(y_i \mid \theta) \right) p(\theta) \, d\theta$$

Economic & Public Health Meaning: Inflationary shocks and regional supply chains define a latent price regime $\theta = (\mu, \sigma^2)$. If $\theta$ were known with certainty, each household's food budget would be modeled as an independent draw from that economic climate. Because $\theta$ is unknown, we integrate over our prior uncertainty about the regional market environment.

02 The Inclusion Vector $I$ and Unit Selection Propensity

For this investigation, we have chosen a survey sample of $n$. To track which citizens are selected and which are not, we create an indicator variable $I$:

$$I_i = \begin{cases} 1 & \text{if unit } i \text{ is selected} \\ 0 & \text{otherwise} \end{cases} \quad \text{for } i = 1, 2, \dots, N$$

In terms of our community food survey ($N = 20{,}000$, $n = 400$):

$I_i = 1$

Resident $i$ is selected into the survey sample (their weekly food spending $y_i$ is observed: $y_i \in y_{\text{obs}}$).

$I_i = 0$

Resident $i$ is not selected and remains un-surveyed (their weekly food spending $y_i$ is missing/unobserved: $y_i \in y_{\text{mis}}$).

Now the question is: how is this vector $I$ selected? Is there an underlying model or specific logic applied to select these households?

For simplicity, we assume that it is selected completely randomly. Under this simple random sampling design, every possible subset of $n$ residents from the population of $N$ residents has the exact same chance of being picked. Therefore, the probability that a particular sample of households is chosen is:

$$p(I \mid y, \phi) = p(I) = \begin{cases} \dbinom{N}{n}^{-1} & \text{if } \sum_{i=1}^N I_i = n \\ 0 & \text{otherwise} \end{cases}$$

Deconstructing the Formula:

  • Why $\binom{N}{n}^{-1}$? There are $\binom{N}{n}$ possible distinct sample combinations of size $n$ from population $N$. In a clean, fair lottery, every possible combination has the exact same uniform probability.
  • Why "0 otherwise"? Because the survey design mandates drawing exactly $n$ people. Any configuration where $\sum_{i=1}^N I_i \neq n$ is impossible.
  • Why does $y$ drop out? The sampling mechanism does not depend on the outcome values $y$ (food spending). Because $p(I \mid y) = p(I)$, the sampling is strongly ignorable by design!

While the joint probability of drawing any specific sample configuration $I$ is $\binom{N}{n}^{-1}$, the probability of getting selected in the sample for each person (or unit $i$) is called the propensity $\pi$ (or unit inclusion propensity $\pi_i$):

$$\pi_i = \Pr(I_i = 1) = \frac{n}{N}$$

In our community survey with $N = 20{,}000$ residents and a sample of $n = 400$, each individual citizen has an identical propensity of $\pi = \frac{400}{20{,}000} = \frac{1}{50} = 2.0\%$.

First-Principles Proof: The Unit-Level Survey Selection Probability is $\pi_i = \frac{n}{N}$

While the joint probability of drawing this exact 400-person sample is $p(I) = \binom{N}{n}^{-1}$, what is the chance that one specific resident (Citizen #7) is surveyed?

Method 1: Combinatorial Team Counting

If Citizen #7 is selected into the survey batch, $(n-1)$ spots remain to be chosen from the $(N-1)$ other residents in the community:

$$\Pr(\text{Citizen } i) = \frac{\binom{N-1}{n-1}}{\binom{N}{n}} = \frac{\frac{(N-1)!}{(n-1)!(N-n)!}}{\frac{N!}{n!(N-n)!}}$$

The $(N-n)!$ terms cancel cleanly: $$\frac{(N-1)!}{N!} \times \frac{n!}{(n-1)!} = \frac{1}{N} \times n = \mathbf{\frac{n}{N}}$$ For our community: $\pi_i = \frac{400}{20{,}000} = 2.0\%$.

Method 2: Symmetry (Feynman's View)

Picture $N$ citizens in the municipal registry and $n$ interview slots in the survey team's schedule:

  • Every citizen has the identical probability $p$ of being interviewed.
  • The expected sum of surveyed citizens is $N \times p$.
  • We know with certainty that exactly $n$ citizens are interviewed: $$N \cdot p = n \implies \mathbf{p = \frac{n}{N}}$$

03 Observed vs. Missing Data: The Split Ledger Formula (Equation 8.5)

Now, we take those selected households where $I_i = 1$ and look at their actual food expenditure. We call these $y_{\text{obs}}$ (the observed values).

Our ultimate objective is to estimate the population mean weekly food expenditure $\bar{y}$ across the entire community using this sample. However, we do not have the food expenditure for the un-surveyed residents. Therefore, we divide our entire population into two distinct groups:

Group 1: Observed Data ($y_{\text{obs}}$)

The $n = 400$ residents who completed the weekly food diary ($I_i = 1$). Using $y_{\text{obs}}$, we can immediately compute their exact sample mean:

$$\bar{y}_{\text{obs}} = \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i: I_i = 1} y_i$$

This is an observed fact with zero posterior variance.

Group 2: Missing Data ($y_{\text{mis}}$)

The $N - n = 19{,}600$ residents living in the community who were not surveyed ($I_i = 0$). If we knew their actual food spending, we could also compute their mean:

$$\bar{y}_{\text{mis}} = \frac{1}{N - n}\sum_{i: I_i = 0} y_i$$

All our uncertainty about $\bar{y}$ stems entirely from this unknown term.

If we knew both $\bar{y}_{\text{obs}}$ and $\bar{y}_{\text{mis}}$, the entire community average food expenditure $\bar{y}$ is simply the weighted combination of the two groups:

$$\bar{y} = \frac{\sum_{i=1}^N y_i}{N} = \frac{\sum_{i: I_i = 1} y_i + \sum_{i: I_i = 0} y_i}{N} = \left(\frac{n}{N}\right) \bar{y}_{\text{obs}} + \left(\frac{N - n}{N}\right) \bar{y}_{\text{mis}} \tag{8.5}$$

The Core Bayesian Insight: Notice what Gelman does here. Estimating the finite population mean $\bar{y}$ is not an abstract inferential dilemma about hypothetical repeated universes. It is strictly an out-of-sample prediction problem. We already know $\bar{y}_{\text{obs}}$ with $100\%$ certainty; finding the posterior distribution of $\bar{y}$ reduces completely to finding the predictive distribution of the un-surveyed residents' food spending $\bar{y}_{\text{mis}}$!

The Epistemic Bridge: Using $y_{\text{obs}}$ to Learn the Context $\theta$

Now we have observed food expenditure data from the $n = 400$ surveyed residents ($y_{\text{obs}}$). What is their primary job in the Bayesian framework?

They act as a telescope into the latent food market context $\theta = (\mu, \sigma^2)$. The 400 grocery diaries reveal the prevailing regional inflation rate, supermarket markups, and dietary costs across the whole municipality. Applying Bayes' rule, we invert the generative process to obtain the posterior distribution of the economic environment:

$$p(\theta \mid y_{\text{obs}}) \propto p(\theta) \prod_{i: I_i = 1} p(y_i \mid \theta)$$

Once this posterior distribution $p(\theta \mid y_{\text{obs}})$ is locked in, we no longer need to speculate about the unobserved households in the abstract. We can use this distribution to generate plausible replicas of the $19{,}600$ un-surveyed citizens' weekly food purchases.

The Two-Stage Generative Monte Carlo Pipeline (2,000 Iterations)

The Computer Simulation Algorithm (BDA3 pp. 205–206)

How do public health analysts compute the complete posterior distribution of community-wide food spending? They program a Monte Carlo loop with $S = 2{,}000$ iterations:

1

Sample a Market State of the World: $\theta^{(s)} \sim p(\theta \mid y_{\text{obs}})$

Conditioned on the $400$ audited weekly food diaries, draw a plausible economic parameter vector $\theta^{(s)} = (\mu^{(s)}, \sigma^{2(s)})$.

Intuition: In draw $s=1$, food inflation might be slightly higher than expected ($\mu^{(1)} = \$54$). In draw $s=2$, slightly milder ($\mu^{(2)} = \$48$). This step absorbs all parameter uncertainty (the fact that surveying 400 residents did not reveal the true market average with infinite precision).

2

Populate the $19{,}600$ Un-Surveyed Citizens: $y_{\text{mis}}^{(s)} \sim p(y \mid \theta^{(s)})$

Fix the simulated price state $\theta^{(s)}$. Now imagine visiting the $N - n = 19{,}600$ un-surveyed homes across the district ($I_i = 0$). Draw a weekly food expenditure for each missing household independently from the conditional likelihood:

$$y_i^{(s)} \sim p(y \mid \theta^{(s)}) \quad \text{for all } i \text{ with } I_i = 0$$

Then average them to obtain the simulated unobserved food spending mean for universe $s$:

$$\bar{y}_{\text{mis}}^{(s)} = \frac{1}{N - n}\sum_{i: I_i = 0} y_i^{(s)}$$

Intuition: Even if the market average $\mu^{(s)}$ is known, individual families experience idiosyncratic shocks (a family gathering, buying infant formula, dietary preferences). This step absorbs all finite sample scatter uncertainty.

3

Recombine via the Split Ledger (Equation 8.5)

Plug the verified sample average $\bar{y}_{\text{obs}}$ and the simulated candidate $\bar{y}_{\text{mis}}^{(s)}$ directly into Equation (8.5):

$$\bar{y}^{(s)} = \left(\frac{n}{N}\right) \bar{y}_{\text{obs}} + \left(\frac{N - n}{N}\right) \bar{y}_{\text{mis}}^{(s)}$$

Multiplying by the population size $N = 20{,}000$ yields the total community weekly food expenditure for draw $s$:

$$\text{Total Food Expenditure}^{(s)} = N \cdot \bar{y}^{(s)} = n\,\bar{y}_{\text{obs}} + (N - n)\,\bar{y}_{\text{mis}}^{(s)}$$

District Baseline: $N \cdot \bar{y}_{\text{obs}} = 20{,}000 \times \$50 = \mathbf{\$1{,}000{,}000}$ ($\mathbf{1\text{ Million dollars}}$ or $\mathbf{\$1\text{ Mil}}$ per week).

Public Policy Engine

The Payoff: 2,000 Simulated Worlds Answer Real Policy Questions

To make public health policy believable, we must ground the numbers in human reality. An average food expenditure of $\bar{y}_{\text{obs}} = \$50$ per week ($\approx \$200$ per month per person, or $\$800$ per month for a family of four) translates across all $N = 20{,}000$ residents into a total weekly community food economy of $\$1{,}000{,}000$ ($1\text{ Million dollars}$ or $\$1\text{ Mil}$) per week.

Furthermore, a public policy officer does not buy groceries for the entire city—affluent and middle-class families pay for their own food. Instead, the policy officer's mandate is targeted social protection: identifying vulnerable families suffering from food insecurity and funding an Emergency Food Voucher Assistance Program to bridge the nutrition gap.

The Classical Policy Bottleneck

Classical survey statistics only easily outputs linear summaries: the overall mean $\bar{y}_{\text{obs}}$ and its standard error $\text{SE}$.

Why This Paralyzes Policy: A health director cannot set food voucher budgets using the average alone. If grocery spending is skewed by high earners, the sample average looks healthy while thousands of low-income families quietly skip meals. Classical formulas cannot easily evaluate non-linear subsidy policies or count the number of families below a subsistence cutoff!

The Bayesian Policy Advantage

The Monte Carlo loop gives the policy officer something far more actionable than an abstract formula: 2,000 fully completed virtual communities stored on their computer:

$$\text{Completed Population Matrix: } 20{,}000 \text{ citizens } \times 2{,}000 \text{ simulated policy worlds}$$

In each column, the 400 surveyed residents keep their audited receipts, while the 19,600 un-surveyed residents are realistically simulated, capturing both macro-inflation uncertainty and idiosyncratic household variation.

How Three Crucial Policy Decisions Become Trivial One-Line Calculations:

1. The Food Poverty Headcount: How Many Citizens Are Hungry? Vulnerability Census

The Policy Dilemma: Public health guidelines define the Nutritional Subsistence Minimum as $\$35$ per week. Anyone spending less than $\$35$ is in acute food distress (skipping meals or relying on nutrient-deficient filler). The director must know: How many residents in our district are currently food insecure?

The Bayesian Solution: In each of the 2,000 simulated worlds $s$, count how many of the $N = 20{,}000$ citizens have $y_i^{(s)} < \$35$: $$H^{(s)} = \sum_{i=1}^N \mathbb{I}\left(y_i^{(s)} < \$35\right)$$ Sorting these 2,000 counts immediately gives the exact posterior distribution of hungry residents (e.g. median $3{,}820$ citizens, with a 95% credible interval of $[3{,}410, \; 4{,}260]$). The director knows precisely how many food parcel kits and soup kitchen rations to stock.

2. The Targeted Food Voucher Budget: Financing the Safety Net Non-Linear "Hockey-Stick" Subsidy

The Policy Dilemma: The city does not buy groceries for everyone; it operates an Emergency Food Voucher Top-Up Program. For any resident whose weekly food spend falls below the healthy $\$50$ baseline, the city issues a voucher covering the exact shortfall: $$v_i^{(s)} = \max\left(0, \; \$50 - y_i^{(s)}\right)$$ (A resident spending $\$65$ receives $\$0$; a resident spending $\$30$ receives a $\$20$ voucher). What is the total weekly municipal voucher budget required: $$V^{(s)} = \sum_{i=1}^N v_i^{(s)}$$

The Bayesian Solution: Because of the sharp $\max(0, \cdot)$ kink, classical linear sampling theory has no closed-form standard error. In the Bayesian simulation, the analyst evaluates this non-linear function across all 2,000 worlds in a single line of code (`np.maximum(0, 50 - y).sum()`), obtaining the complete posterior distribution of municipal voucher payouts (median: $\$102{,}400$; 95% interval: $[\$89{,}200, \; \$116{,}800]$).

3. Solvency & 99% Stress Reserve: Will the Emergency Relief Fund Run Dry? Cabinet / City Treasury

The Policy Dilemma: The City Council allocated an emergency weekly food voucher contingency reserve of $C_{\text{voucher}} = \$115{,}000$. The Health Director must answer: What is the exact probability that actual voucher claims will exceed this contingency reserve?

The Bayesian Solution: Count how many of the 2,000 simulated worlds required voucher funding $V^{(s)} > \$115{,}000$. If 312 worlds breached it: $$\Pr(\text{Voucher Fund Deficit}) = \frac{312}{2{,}000} = 15.6\%$$ Furthermore, reading off the 99th percentile ($V_{0.99} = \$121{,}800$) tells the City Treasurer the exact stress reserve required so that the voucher program never has to turn away a struggling family, even in the worst 1-in-100 food inflation crisis.

Generalizing Beyond the Mean

Evaluating Any Arbitrary Population Quantity $Q(y)$ for Public Policy

In classical survey sampling, estimating quantities beyond the linear average $\bar{y}$ requires complex mathematical acrobatics: Woodruff approximations for medians, Taylor series linearization for ratios, or bootstrap resamples.

Under the Bayesian simulation paradigm, however, every policy estimand is evaluated identically. Because each iteration $s$ yields a completed finite population vector of all $N = 20{,}000$ citizens:

$$y^{(s)} = \left( y_1, \; y_2, \; \dots, \; y_{400}, \; y_{401}^{(s)}, \; \dots, \; y_{20{,}000}^{(s)} \right)$$

The policy officer simply evaluates their target function $Q(y^{(s)})$ directly on the $N = 20{,}000$ completed values:

1. The Headcount Poverty Rate: $P_0(y^{(s)})$

The percentage of citizens living in nutritional subsistence deficit:

$$P_0^{(s)} = \frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^N \mathbb{I}\left(y_i^{(s)} < \$35\right)$$

In each draw $s$, compute this percentage. The resulting 2,000 values give the mayor an honest credible interval on the district's true poverty rate (e.g. $19.1\% \pm 2.1\%$).

2. Poverty Depth (The Foster-Greer-Thorbecke Index $P_1(y^{(s)})$)

It is not enough to know how many people are hungry; policy officers need to know how severe their hunger is:

$$P_1^{(s)} = \frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^N \frac{\max\left(0, \; \$35 - y_i^{(s)}\right)}{\$35}$$

Quantifies the average percentage depth below the subsistence poverty line.

3. The Population Median: $\text{median}(y^{(s)})$

If a small wealthy enclave in the district spends $\$300/\text{week}$ on luxury groceries, the arithmetic mean $\bar{y}$ will be dragged upward to $\$50$, giving a false illusion of community affluence. The median:

$$M^{(s)} = \text{median}\left(y_1, \dots, y_{400}, y_{401}^{(s)}, \dots, y_{20{,}000}^{(s)}\right)$$

Reveals what the typical resident actually spends (often closer to $\$42$), protecting the health director from misleading averages.

4. Severe Food Burden Ratio: $R(y^{(s)})$

Economists define high food stress when a household spends more than 30% of its total income on nutrition:

$$R^{(s)} = \frac{\sum_{i=1}^N y_i^{(s)}}{\sum_{i=1}^N \text{Income}_i}$$

Computing this ratio on each simulated ledger gives the exact probability distribution of systemic cost-of-living distress.

The Core Takeaway: Once you have filled in the missing entries of the finite population ledger, any policy question—no matter how non-linear, censored, discontinuous, or mathematically intricate—is answered with a simple, transparent one-line function across the 2,000 completed vectors.

04 Analytical Derivation: The Finite Population Correction (Equation 8.6)

When the survey sample $n$ and the un-surveyed balance $N - n$ are reasonably large, we do not even need to run computer simulations to find the posterior distribution of the population mean food expenditure $\bar{y}$. We can derive it analytically from first principles.

Step 1 of 5

Start with the Physical Object: The Un-Surveyed Average

Look at the physical target we must predict. In our community of $N = 20{,}000$ residents, we collected food expenditure diaries from $n = 400$ citizens. Remaining in the community are exactly $N - n = 19{,}600$ residents where $I_i = 0$.

The average weekly food expenditure of these un-surveyed residents is simply their arithmetic mean:

$$\bar{y}_{\text{mis}} = \frac{1}{N - n}\sum_{i: I_i = 0} y_i$$

Notice that this is not an abstract superpopulation parameter or an imaginary asymptote. It is a concrete, physical number sitting right now in the households of our district: $\frac{y_{401} + y_{402} + \dots + y_{20{,}000}}{19{,}600}$. If we had the municipal budget to visit all $19{,}600$ remaining residents and audit their grocery receipts, $\bar{y}_{\text{mis}}$ is the exact dollar amount that would appear at the bottom.

The entire mathematical task of this section is simply: What is our probability distribution over this physical sum?

Step 2 of 5

Applying the Central Limit Theorem: Freezing the Economic Climate ($\theta$)

Whenever you add up thousands of random quantities ($N - n = 19{,}600$), the Central Limit Theorem (CLT) immediately comes to mind.

Wait—can we apply the Central Limit Theorem directly to the residents' food expenditures?
No. The CLT requires the items being added to be independent. Real household grocery spending is not independent across people: if municipal grocery and fuel price inflation spikes, food expenditures surge across the entire community simultaneously.

To unlock the CLT, we do what we established in Section 1: we freeze the underlying economic climate and food price index $\theta$.

The Oracle's View: Conditioning on $\theta$

Suppose an omniscient Oracle whispers the true economic climate $\theta$ into your ear. As our graphical model proved, conditional on $\theta$, the individual food expenditures become conditionally independent.

Given that fixed state $\theta$, each resident's weekly food spend $y_i$ has an implied mean $\mu(\theta) = E(y_i \mid \theta)$ and variance $\sigma^2(\theta) = \text{var}(y_i \mid \theta)$.

By the Central Limit Theorem, the average of $N - n = 19{,}600$ independent random variables must be approximately Normal, defined by its expected value and its variance:

$$p(\bar{y}_{\text{mis}} \mid \theta) \approx \mathcal{N}\Big( E(\bar{y}_{\text{mis}} \mid \theta), \; \text{var}(\bar{y}_{\text{mis}} \mid \theta) \Big)$$

Now we calculate each of these two components directly from the physical definition $\bar{y}_{\text{mis}} = \frac{1}{N - n}\sum_{i: I_i = 0} y_i$:

1. The Mean: $E(\bar{y}_{\text{mis}} \mid \theta)$

Pass the expectation inside the linear sum:

$$E\left[\frac{1}{N - n}\sum_{i: I_i = 0} y_i \;\middle|\; \theta\right] = \frac{1}{N - n}\sum_{i: I_i = 0} \mu(\theta)$$

Adding $(N - n)$ copies of $\mu(\theta)$: $$\frac{(N - n)\,\mu(\theta)}{N - n} = \mathbf{\mu(\theta)}$$

2. The Variance: $\text{var}(\bar{y}_{\text{mis}} \mid \theta)$

Because individual food spends are independent given $\theta$, variances add and the constant $\frac{1}{N - n}$ pulls out squared:

$$\text{var}\left(\frac{1}{N - n}\sum_{i: I_i = 0} y_i \;\middle|\; \theta\right) = \frac{1}{(N - n)^2}\sum_{i: I_i = 0} \sigma^2(\theta)$$

Adding $(N - n)$ copies of $\sigma^2(\theta)$: $$\frac{(N - n)\,\sigma^2(\theta)}{(N - n)^2} = \mathbf{\frac{1}{N - n}\sigma^2(\theta)}$$

Plugging these two calculated values into our Gaussian distribution yields Gelman's exact conditional formula:

$$p(\bar{y}_{\text{mis}} \mid \theta) \approx \mathcal{N}\left(\bar{y}_{\text{mis}} \;\middle|\; \mu(\theta), \; \frac{1}{N - n}\sigma^2(\theta)\right)$$
Step 3 of 5

The Real World: Integrating Out $\theta$ (A Normal Mixture of Normals)

In reality, the Oracle leaves the room: we do not know $\theta$. We only have our $n = 400$ surveyed food diaries ($y_{\text{obs}}$).

To find the true predictive distribution given our data, the Law of Total Probability tells us to average over all possible values of $\theta$, weighted by its posterior distribution:

$$p(\bar{y}_{\text{mis}} \mid y_{\text{obs}}) = \int p(\bar{y}_{\text{mis}} \mid \theta) \, p(\theta \mid y_{\text{obs}}) \, d\theta$$

Because $n = 400$ is also reasonably large, the posterior distribution of $\theta$ is approximately normal. A normal distribution integrated against another normal distribution creates a normal mixture of normals, which is itself approximately Normal:

$$\bar{y}_{\text{mis}} \mid y_{\text{obs}} \approx \mathcal{N}\Big( E(\bar{y}_{\text{mis}} \mid y_{\text{obs}}), \; \text{var}(\bar{y}_{\text{mis}} \mid y_{\text{obs}}) \Big)$$

A normal distribution is completely defined by two numbers: its Mean (Center) and its Variance (Spread). What are they?

A. Deriving the Center: The Law of Total Expectation

We want to find $E(\bar{y}_{\text{mis}} \mid y_{\text{obs}})$. By the Law of Total Expectation (iterated averages), we condition first on the latent parameters $\theta$, and then take the expectation over the posterior distribution of $\theta$:

$$E(\bar{y}_{\text{mis}} \mid y_{\text{obs}}) = E\Big[ E(\bar{y}_{\text{mis}} \mid \theta, y_{\text{obs}}) \;\Big|\; y_{\text{obs}} \Big] = E\Big[ \mu(\theta) \;\Big|\; y_{\text{obs}} \Big]$$

Under standard uninformative priors (or large-sample posterior normality for $n = 400$), the posterior mean of the latent parameter $\mu(\theta)$ given the survey sample is the sample mean $\bar{y}_{\text{obs}}$:

$$E(\bar{y}_{\text{mis}} \mid y_{\text{obs}}) \approx \mathbf{\bar{y}_{\text{obs}}}$$

Physical Meaning: Our single best estimate for the average food spend of the $19{,}600$ un-surveyed residents living in the district is simply the average of the $400$ food diaries we audited in our survey.

B. Deriving the Spread: The Law of Total Variance

Now we evaluate $\text{var}(\bar{y}_{\text{mis}} \mid y_{\text{obs}})$. By the Law of Total Variance (Eve's Law), the total variance of $\bar{y}_{\text{mis}}$ decomposes into two additive, physically independent components:

$$\text{var}(\bar{y}_{\text{mis}} \mid y_{\text{obs}}) = \underbrace{\text{var}\Big( E[\bar{y}_{\text{mis}} \mid \theta] \;\Big|\; y_{\text{obs}} \Big)}_{\text{Variance of Expected Value}} + \underbrace{E\Big[ \text{var}(\bar{y}_{\text{mis}} \mid \theta) \;\Big|\; y_{\text{obs}} \Big]}_{\text{Expected Value of Variance}}$$

Substitute our two known conditional results from Step 2 ($E[\bar{y}_{\text{mis}} \mid \theta] = \mu(\theta)$ and $\text{var}(\bar{y}_{\text{mis}} \mid \theta) = \frac{\sigma^2(\theta)}{N - n}$):

$$\text{var}(\bar{y}_{\text{mis}} \mid y_{\text{obs}}) = \text{var}\big(\mu(\theta) \mid y_{\text{obs}}\big) + E\left[ \frac{\sigma^2(\theta)}{N - n} \;\middle|\; y_{\text{obs}} \right]$$

Now examine each of these two terms:

  1. Term 1 (Economic Climate / Parameter Uncertainty): $$\text{var}\big(\mu(\theta) \mid y_{\text{obs}}\big) \approx \frac{s^2_{\text{obs}}}{n}$$ This reflects our uncertainty about the true district-wide underlying mean $\mu(\theta)$ after surveying only $n = 400$ citizens. Notice that even if the un-surveyed population were infinite ($N \to \infty$), this term never goes away because we only ever collected 400 food diaries.
  2. Term 2 (Finite Sample Scatter): Pulling the constant scalar $\frac{1}{N - n}$ outside the expectation: $$E\left[ \frac{\sigma^2(\theta)}{N - n} \;\middle|\; y_{\text{obs}} \right] = \frac{1}{N - n} E\big[\sigma^2(\theta) \mid y_{\text{obs}}\big] \approx \frac{s^2_{\text{obs}}}{N - n}$$ This reflects the fact that even if an Oracle revealed $\mu(\theta)$ with 100% certainty, the $19{,}600$ un-surveyed citizens in the community are finite in number and still fluctuate around $\mu(\theta)$ due to idiosyncratic household dietary preferences and family sizes.

Adding these two independent variances yields:

$$\text{var}(\bar{y}_{\text{mis}} \mid y_{\text{obs}}) \approx \frac{s^2_{\text{obs}}}{n} + \frac{s^2_{\text{obs}}}{N - n} = s^2_{\text{obs}}\left( \frac{1}{n} + \frac{1}{N - n} \right) = \mathbf{\frac{N}{n(N - n)} s^2_{\text{obs}}}$$

Combining the Mean and Variance, the complete unconditional predictive distribution for the un-surveyed residents is:

$$\bar{y}_{\text{mis}} \mid y_{\text{obs}} \approx \mathcal{N}\left( \bar{y}_{\text{obs}}, \; \frac{N}{n(N - n)} s^2_{\text{obs}} \right)$$
Step 4 of 5

Scaling Onto the Full Population Mean Food Spend $\bar{y}$ (Equation 8.6)

Now we return to Gelman's Split Ledger (Equation 8.5) to find the variance of the entire population mean weekly food expenditure $\bar{y}$:

$$\bar{y} = \left(\frac{n}{N}\right) \bar{y}_{\text{obs}} + \left(\frac{N - n}{N}\right) \bar{y}_{\text{mis}}$$

Since $\bar{y}_{\text{obs}}$ is a verified survey fact with zero posterior variance, the constant weight $\frac{N - n}{N}$ pulls out squared:

$$\text{var}(\bar{y} \mid y_{\text{obs}}) = \left(\frac{N - n}{N}\right)^2 \text{var}(\bar{y}_{\text{mis}} \mid y_{\text{obs}})$$

Substitute our formula for $\text{var}(\bar{y}_{\text{mis}} \mid y_{\text{obs}})$:

$$= \frac{(N - n)^2}{N^2} \times \left[ \frac{N}{n(N - n)} s^2_{\text{obs}} \right]$$

Watch $(N - n)$ and $N$ cancel cleanly:

$$= \left(\frac{N - n}{N}\right) \frac{s^2_{\text{obs}}}{n} = \left(1 - \frac{n}{N}\right) \frac{s^2_{\text{obs}}}{n} = \mathbf{\left(\frac{1}{n} - \frac{1}{N}\right) s^2_{\text{obs}}}$$
Step 5 of 5: The Master Formula

The Complete Normal Approximation (BDA3 Equation 8.6)

$$\bar{y} \mid y_{\text{obs}} \approx \mathcal{N}\left( \bar{y}_{\text{obs}}, \; \left(\frac{1}{n} - \frac{1}{N}\right) s^2_{\text{obs}} \right)$$
The Two Boundary Realities (Feynman Sanity Check):
  • What if $N \to \infty$ (Infinite Population)? The term $\frac{1}{N} \to 0$, giving $\text{var}(\bar{y}) = \frac{s^2_{\text{obs}}}{n}$. We recover the standard infinite-population Central Limit Theorem without modification!
  • What if $n = N$ (Complete Census)? Then $\frac{1}{n} - \frac{1}{N} = 0$, giving $\text{var}(\bar{y}) = 0$. Once every single citizen in the community has logged their food expenditures, all uncertainty vanishes—because you know the truth!

05 The Real Situation: Frequentist Limitations vs. The Bayesian Decision Engine

Why does this mathematical distinction between superpopulations and finite populations matter in the real world?

To see why Bayesian statistics is uniquely suited for decision analysis and high-stakes policy governance, consider the actual municipal dilemma faced by a City Public Health Director and Social Welfare Board.

The Public Health Dilemma

The Community Food Security & Emergency Cushion Decision

The municipal district is home to $N = 20{,}000$ citizens. Amid rising food costs, the Department of Public Health surveyed a random sample of $n = 400$ household food diaries, observing an average weekly spend of $\bar{y}_{\text{obs}} = \$50$ per person ($s_{\text{obs}} = \$50$).

Believable Scale Check: An individual food spend of $\$50$ per week ($\approx \$200$ per month, or $\$800$ per month for a family of four) is a realistic baseline budget. Across the entire district of $N = 20{,}000$ citizens, this translates into a total community food expenditure of $T = 20{,}000 \times \$50 = \$1{,}000{,}000$ per week ($1\text{ Million dollars}$ or $\$1\text{ Mil}$).

The City Council has appropriated an emergency cost-of-living buffer capped at $C = \$1{,}050{,}000$ ($1.05\text{ Mil}$). If community food costs spike beyond this threshold, low-income families are pushed into acute malnutrition, triggering emergency food voucher claims that could bankrupt the social welfare contingency reserve. The Public Health Director must answer three concrete policy questions:

Decision 1: Assistance Deficit Risk "What is the exact probability that community food expenditure needs exceed our $\$1{,}050{,}000$ ($1.05\text{ Mil}$) municipal assistance cushion?"
Decision 2: 99% Stress Reserve "What is our maximum required emergency food relief reserve under a 99% severe cost-of-living stress scenario?"
Decision 3: Budget Rebalancing "Can we safely release surplus emergency contingency funds to primary health clinics without risking a voucher deficit?"
Traditional Approach What Most People Do

The Frequentist Method

Computes point estimate $\widehat{T} = N \bar{y}_{\text{obs}} = \$1{,}000{,}000$ ($1\text{ Mil}$) and standard error $\text{SE} = N \sqrt{\frac{1}{n} - \frac{1}{N}} s_{\text{obs}} = \$49{,}500$. Generates a 95% Confidence Interval: $[\$903{,}000, \; \$1{,}097{,}000]$ ($[\$0.90\text{M}, \; \$1.10\text{M}]$).

Question 1: Probability of Budget Breach

"Cannot calculate." In frequentist theory, the total community spend $T$ is a fixed unknown constant, not a random variable. It either exceeds $\$1{,}050{,}000$ ($1.05\text{ Mil}$) or it doesn't—you cannot assign it an epistemic probability.

Question 2: Meaning of the Interval

The 95% refers to hypothetical repeated surveys across imaginary parallel universes. It cannot tell the Director whether this specific district's food assistance will fall short this week.

Decision Verdict

Policy makers are left in limbo: no probability metric to evaluate whether a budget breach is an acceptable risk.

Bayesian Decision Engine Actionable for Leadership

The Bayesian Predictive Method

Treats the $19{,}600$ un-surveyed citizens as physical missing data and forms an exact posterior predictive distribution for total community food expenditure: $T = n\bar{y}_{\text{obs}} + (N - n)\bar{y}_{\text{mis}}$.

Question 1: Probability of Budget Breach

Exact, direct probability: $\Pr(\text{Total Community Spend} > \$1{,}050{,}000 \mid \text{data}) = \mathbf{15.6\%}$. The Director can immediately evaluate whether a 15.6% risk of exhausting the emergency fund is acceptable.

Question 2: 99% Emergency Food Buffer

Read off the exact 99th percentile of total community food spend: $\mathbf{\$1{,}114{,}900}$ ($\mathbf{\$1.11\text{ Mil}}$). Tells the city treasurer precisely how much funding guarantees 99% emergency coverage.

Decision Verdict

Clear, actionable governance: approved or bolstered with mathematical precision.
