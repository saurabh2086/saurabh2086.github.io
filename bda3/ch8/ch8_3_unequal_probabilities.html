Unequal Probabilities of Selection

When cluster sizes are unknown, why simple group averages mislead us, how attendance habits bias our data, and how a joint Bayesian model makes sense of the Alcoholics Anonymous survey.

Saurabh Kumar Pandey • Gelman et al., BDA3 §8.3
Reading Time: ~12 mins • Study Notes

The Loss of the Registry: When Cluster Sizes are Unknown

In the Melbourne schoolchildren study, our analysis relied on a really convenient administrative detail: we knew the exact size of every cluster in the city.

Even for the nine hundred schools we never visited, the Department of Education had an official registry listing their total classroom counts ($M_k$) and student enrollments ($N_k$). When we needed to simulate the unobserved schools to estimate the citywide average, we could just plug those known numbers straight into our regression.

In a lot of real-world surveys, though, we don't have that luxury at all:

  • Informal Support Networks: Mutual aid groups, bereavement circles, and addiction recovery programs keep no roll calls or rosters.
  • Public Health Drop-In Clinics: Needle exchanges, mobile health vans, and rural outreach centers have fluctuating daily attendance with no master lists.
  • Community & Religious Gatherings: Congregations and neighborhood associations know how many physical meeting spots exist, but have no accurate counts of how many people belong to each.

This is the exact problem in the nationwide triennial survey of Alcoholics Anonymous (AA) discussed at the end of Section 8.3.

The Fundamental Challenge: How do you estimate the true proportion of members across an entire nation when you don't even know how many people belong to the thousands of groups you never visited?

How Selection Actually Works: The 5-Step Real-World Funnel

In theory, a fair survey is simple: every person should have the exact same chance of being picked.

$$\Pr(\text{Person } i \text{ is picked}) = \text{Constant for everyone}$$

When this is true, the sample is a fair mirror of the population. You just average the numbers, and you are done.

In the real world, this never happens.

In the national Alcoholics Anonymous survey, nobody flipped a coin for each individual member. Instead, for someone's response to end up in our dataset, it had to pass through a chain of five real-world steps:

Step 1
Random process picks a group

Headquarters randomly draws 400 groups out of 6,400 across the country. Groups have an equal chance, but individual people do not.

Step 2
Group size ($x_j$) sets the local pool

Some groups have 10 regular members; others have 100. Being 1 in 10 gives you a much larger presence in your group than being 1 in 100.

Step 3
Day and weather impact attendance

Surveys are handed out on one particular day. A snowy Tuesday morning draws a completely different crowd than a clear Friday evening.

Step 4
Personal context impacts attendance

A newcomer in acute distress may go every single day. A veteran with 20 years of sobriety may go once a month. Someone attending 7 days a week is 7 times more likely to be in the room on survey day!

Step 5
People context impacts survey response

Sitting in the room is not enough—not everyone responds to the survey. A person's own context determines both whether they fill out the form ($n_j \approx \pi x_j$) and what they answer.

The Big Takeaway: We did not sample people with equal probability. Both attendance and response are driven by the group pool, day and weather, and people's personal context.

Computing the Probability of Selection ($\pi_i$)

We can now use this causal diagram to compute the exact probability $\pi_i$ that any individual person $i$ (in group $j$) ends up in our survey dataset.

Following the arrows from top to bottom, three events must happen in sequence:

$$\begin{aligned} \pi_i &= \Pr(\text{Person } i \text{ responds}) \\[8pt] &= \underbrace{\Pr(\text{Group } j \text{ selected})}_{\text{Step 1: Random Lottery}} \;\times\; \underbrace{\Pr(\text{Attends}_i \mid \text{Group } j, \text{Day}, \text{Context}_i)}_{\text{Steps 2–4: Attendance Filter}} \;\times\; \underbrace{\Pr(\text{Responds}_i \mid \text{Attends}_i, \text{Context}_i)}_{\text{Step 5: Response Filter}} \end{aligned}$$

Look closely at the three terms on the right:

  • Term 1 — Group Selection ($\frac{400}{6400} = \frac{1}{16}$): This first term is the only part that is equal for everyone.
  • Term 2 — Attendance Probability ($p_{\text{attend}, i}$): Driven by Day & Weather and Personal Context. A daily attendee has $p_{\text{attend}, i} = \frac{7}{7} = 1.0$, whereas a bi-weekly attendee has $p_{\text{attend}, i} = \frac{1}{14} \approx 0.071$.
  • Term 3 — Response Probability ($p_{\text{resp}, i}$): Also driven by Personal Context. An engaged member may have $p_{\text{resp}, i} = 0.80$, while a private or hurried member may have $p_{\text{resp}, i} = 0.40$.

A 2-Person Concrete Example: Why $\pi_i$ Varies Wildly

Let us plug in the numbers for two different members—Alice (a newcomer in crisis) and Bob (a 10-year sober veteran)—to see how different their selection probabilities really are:

Member & Context 1. Group Picked 2. Attends on Day 3. Fills Form Net Probability ($\pi_i$)
Alice (Newcomer) Sober 2 mos; attends daily $\frac{1}{16}$ ($6.25\%$) $\frac{7}{7}$ ($100\%$) $0.80$ ($80\%$) $0.0500$ ($5.00\%$)
Bob (Veteran) Sober 10 yrs; attends bi-weekly $\frac{1}{16}$ ($6.25\%$) $\frac{1}{14}$ ($7.1\%$) $0.40$ ($40\%$) $0.0018$ ($0.18\%$)

The $28\times$ Distortion: Even though Alice's group and Bob's group had the exact same $\frac{1}{16}$ chance of being selected, Alice's personal probability of landing in the dataset ($\pi_{\text{Alice}} = 5.0\%$) is 28 times higher than Bob's ($\pi_{\text{Bob}} = 0.18\%$).

Because People Context simultaneously drives who shows up, who responds, and whether they have been sober for more than 5 years, each person has a completely different $\pi_i$. If we simply average the raw survey responses, our estimate will be heavily biased toward members like Alice.

Making Sense of a Biased Survey

So what should we do? How do we make sense of a survey when the data is this biased?

Let's walk through it step by step—starting from the best-case scenario and seeing what the real world forces us to adjust.

1. What Constitutes "Everyone"? (Defining $x_j$)

Picture the best-case scenario first: we ask everyone in Alcoholics Anonymous to take the survey, and everyone responds.

But wait—what constitutes "everyone"?

People leave and join these groups all the time. There is no membership list and no roll call. Because people are constantly flowing in and out, we can only talk about the average number of people who attend each group. Let's call that average attendance $x_j$.

2. Can We Fill In the Missing Values from the Responses We Collected? ($\theta_j$)

Now, we just want to get our estimate—say, the fraction of members who have been sober for more than 5 years. Since we don't have everyone's response, can we fill out the missing values based on the responses we collected?

We could if we assumed the selected people simply represent one big, uniform population.

But these people are segregated into different groups. People in one group are often much more similar to each other than to people in other groups—a morning meeting in a retirement town looks very different from a late-night meeting near a university.

Because groups differ, each group $j$ has its own local proportion $\theta_j$ of people who answer "Yes" (sober $> 5$ years). If we can get the statistics $(x_j, \theta_j)$ of each group, then we can combine them to compute the overall average.

3. Writing Down the Overall Average

Once we have both numbers for each group $j$, the math is straightforward:

  • Group $j$ has an average of $x_j$ people.
  • A fraction $\theta_j$ of them answer "Yes"—which gives $x_j \theta_j$ "Yes" people in group $j$.

To compute the overall average across all $K = 6{,}400$ groups in the country, we add up the "Yes" people across all groups and divide by the total number of people across all groups:

$$\bar{Y} = \frac{\sum_{j=1}^K x_j \theta_j}{\sum_{j=1}^K x_j} = \frac{\text{Total "Yes" members across all } K \text{ groups}}{\text{Total members across all } K \text{ groups}}$$

4. What We Actually Have on Our Desk (The Missing Pieces)

Look at the formula for $\bar{Y}$ above: to evaluate it, we need $(x_j, \theta_j)$ for all $6{,}400$ groups. Compare that to what we actually have in hand:

  • Only 400 groups responded: For the remaining $6{,}000$ unsampled groups, we have zero observations.
  • Even in the 400 sampled groups, we don't see $x_j$ or $\theta_j$ directly: We only see $n_j$ returned questionnaires (around 20 per group) and $y_j$ "Yes" responses, which we can model with a Binomial distribution:
$$y_j \sim \text{Binomial}(n_j, \theta_j)$$

A Quick Note on Within-Group Sampling: Strictly speaking, sampling $n_j$ people out of a group of size $x_j$ without replacement would involve a finite-population correction. Why can we ignore it here and just use $\text{Binomial}(n_j, \theta_j)$? Because $6{,}000$ entire groups weren't sampled at all! That uncertainty across the unsampled groups is so much larger than any small adjustment inside a single room.

So how do we estimate both the unknown group sizes $x_j$ and the unknown rates $\theta_j$ across all $6{,}400$ groups from just $(n_j, y_j)$ in 400 rooms? That leads directly to our next step: modeling $(x_j, \theta_j)$ together.

Building a Joint Model for $(x_j, \theta_j)$

Look at our target formula again: $\bar{Y} = \frac{\sum x_j \theta_j}{\sum x_j}$. To get the numerator, we multiply each group's size $x_j$ by its "Yes" rate $\theta_j$.

1. Can We Model $x_j$ and $\theta_j$ Separately?

Our first instinct might be to keep things simple: build one model for group sizes $x_j$, build another separate model for group sobriety rates $\theta_j$, and multiply them together.

Wait—what goes wrong if we treat $x_j$ and $\theta_j$ as independent?

Think about how groups work in practice. Larger downtown meetings might have stronger sponsor networks and higher long-term sobriety rates $\theta_j$ than tiny groups—or the opposite could happen if large open meetings attract mostly newcomers.

If $x_j$ and $\theta_j$ are related, then in the sum $\sum x_j \theta_j$, large $x_j$'s will consistently pair up with large (or small) $\theta_j$'s. If we model them separately, we completely miss that correlation. So we need a joint model: $p(x_j, \theta_j)$.

2. Breaking $p(x_j, \theta_j)$ into Two Simple Pieces

Using basic probability, we can factor any joint distribution into two sequential steps:

$$p(x_j, \theta_j) = \underbrace{p(x_j)}_{\text{Step A: Group size}} \;\times\; \underbrace{p(\theta_j \mid x_j)}_{\text{Step B: Rate given size}}$$
  • Step A — Group sizes $p(x_j)$: Across the $6{,}400$ groups, average attendance $x_j$ varies around some overall mean size $\mu_x$ with variance $\sigma_x^2$.
  • Step B — Rate given size $p(\theta_j \mid x_j)$: Given a group's size $x_j$, we let its sobriety rate $\theta_j$ depend linearly on $x_j$ as $\alpha + \beta x_j$, with residual variance $\sigma_\theta^2$.

Writing both steps as Normal distributions gives us our joint prior:

$$\begin{aligned} x_j &\sim \mathcal{N}(\mu_x, \; \sigma_x^2) \\[6pt] \theta_j \mid x_j &\sim \mathcal{N}(\alpha + \beta x_j, \; \sigma_\theta^2) \end{aligned}$$

Why use Normal distributions here?
You might immediately notice something odd: group sizes $x_j$ have to be positive ($x_j > 0$), and probabilities $\theta_j$ have to stay between $0$ and $1$, whereas a Normal distribution stretches from $-\infty$ to $+\infty$. Why use a Normal model anyway?

Because when everything is Normal and linear, we can work out the expectations by hand and see the exact algebraic formula for how group size variation shifts the national estimate. Once we understand how the pieces interact, we can always fit a bounded Log-Normal and Logit-Normal version on a computer.

3. Connecting the Hidden Parameters to Our Observed Data

The prior above describes the underlying parameters $(x_j, \theta_j)$ of each group. Next, we need to connect those hidden numbers to what we actually observe in our 400 returned packets—the "Yes" count $y_j$ and the number of returned forms $n_j$.

We can link them with two approximations:

Bridge 1: From $y_j / n_j$ to $\theta_j$

In group $j$, the observed fraction of "Yes" answers is $\bar{y}_j = y_j / n_j$. For a Binomial count $y_j \sim \text{Bin}(n_j, \theta_j)$, the sample proportion has mean $\theta_j$ and variance $\frac{\theta_j(1-\theta_j)}{n_j}$.

Plugging in the observed fraction $\frac{y_j}{n_j}$ for $\theta_j$ in the variance gives the Normal approximation:

$$\frac{y_j}{n_j} \sim \mathcal{N}(\theta_j, \; \sigma_j^2)$$

Where does $n_j^3$ come from? Notice that $\sigma_j^2 = \frac{(y_j/n_j)(1 - y_j/n_j)}{n_j} = \frac{(y_j/n_j)((n_j - y_j)/n_j)}{n_j} = \frac{y_j(n_j - y_j)}{n_j^3}$. It is just the usual binomial proportion variance written over a common denominator.

Bridge 2: From $n_j$ to $x_j$

How does the number of returned forms $n_j$ tell us about the group's size $x_j$? If $x_j$ people attend on average, and each person responds with probability $\pi$, we expect $\pi x_j$ forms back.

Modeling the count of returned forms as $n_j \sim \text{Poisson}(\pi x_j)$ (where mean equals variance) and using a Normal approximation gives:

$$n_j \sim \mathcal{N}(\pi x_j, \; \pi x_j)$$

The Scaling Role of $\pi$: Each group's returned count $n_j$ gives us a noisy observation of $\pi x_j$, which lets us learn the relative distribution of group sizes across the country.

4. Putting the Two Tracks Together

Combining the joint prior with the two data bridges (and placing a flat prior on the hyperparameters $\alpha, \beta, \sigma_x, \sigma_\theta, \pi$) gives us a two-track hierarchical model:

Now that we have the joint model in place, let's see what happens when we plug it into our target $\bar{Y} = \frac{\sum x_j \theta_j}{\sum x_j}$—something really interesting comes out of the expectation.

Solving for the National Average: How Group Size Variance Shifts the Estimate

We now have all the pieces on the table to evaluate the overall proportion across all $K = 6{,}400$ groups:

$$\bar{Y} = \frac{\sum_{j=1}^K x_j \theta_j}{\sum_{j=1}^K x_j}$$

1. Turning the Sum Over $6{,}400$ Groups into Expected Values

How do we compute a sum over $6{,}400$ groups when $6{,}000$ of them were never visited?

First, divide both the numerator and the denominator by $K$:

$$\bar{Y} = \frac{\frac{1}{K}\sum_{j=1}^K x_j \theta_j}{\frac{1}{K}\sum_{j=1}^K x_j}$$

Now the top is the average of $x_j \theta_j$ across all groups, and the bottom is the average of $x_j$ across all groups. Since $K = 6{,}400$ is large, we can approximate those sample averages over $K$ with their expected values under our model:

$$\bar{Y} \approx \frac{\text{E}(x_j \theta_j)}{\text{E}(x_j)}$$

2. Working Through the Algebra Step by Step

In the book, this ratio jumps straight to the final expression. Let's work out both expectations step by step using our Normal model:

  • Step A — The Denominator $\text{E}(x_j)$:
    Since $x_j \sim \mathcal{N}(\mu_x, \sigma_x^2)$, the expected size of a group is simply: $$\text{E}(x_j) = \mu_x$$
  • Step B — The Numerator $\text{E}(x_j \theta_j)$:
    From $\theta_j \mid x_j \sim \mathcal{N}(\alpha + \beta x_j, \sigma_\theta^2)$, we can write $\theta_j = \alpha + \beta x_j + \epsilon_j$, where the residual $\epsilon_j$ has mean zero ($\text{E}[\epsilon_j \mid x_j] = 0$). Multiplying by $x_j$ and taking the expectation gives: $$\text{E}(x_j \theta_j) = \text{E}\big[\, x_j(\alpha + \beta x_j + \epsilon_j) \,\big] = \alpha \text{E}(x_j) + \beta \text{E}(x_j^2)$$
  • Step C — Where $\sigma_x^2$ Enters:
    What is $\text{E}(x_j^2)$? Using the basic identity $\text{Var}(x_j) = \text{E}(x_j^2) - [\text{E}(x_j)]^2$, we can rewrite it as: $$\text{E}(x_j^2) = \mu_x^2 + \sigma_x^2$$

Plugging $\text{E}(x_j) = \mu_x$ and $\text{E}(x_j^2) = \mu_x^2 + \sigma_x^2$ back into $\bar{Y} = \frac{\text{E}(x_j \theta_j)}{\text{E}(x_j)}$ gives:

$$\bar{Y} = \frac{\alpha \mu_x + \beta(\mu_x^2 + \sigma_x^2)}{\mu_x} = \underbrace{(\alpha + \beta \mu_x)}_{\text{Rate at average group size}} + \;\; \underbrace{\beta \frac{\sigma_x^2}{\mu_x}}_{\text{Size-Variance Correction}}$$

3. Why Does $\beta \frac{\sigma_x^2}{\mu_x}$ Appear Here?

Take a second to look at that second term.

If someone told you the average group size is $\mu_x$, your first guess might be to plug $\mu_x$ into the regression line and say the national average is $\alpha + \beta \mu_x$. Why do we have to add $\beta \frac{\sigma_x^2}{\mu_x}$? Why does the spread in group sizes ($\sigma_x^2$) change the national average?

Let's test this with a simple 2-group example where $\alpha = 0$ and $\beta = 0.01$ (so a group of size $x_j$ has rate $\theta_j = 0.01 x_j$), and the average group size is $\mu_x = 50$:

Physical Intuition Check: Equal Group Sizes ($\sigma_x^2 = 0$) vs. Unequal Group Sizes ($\sigma_x^2 > 0$)
Scenario ($\mu_x = 50$ in both) Group 1 Group 2 Size Variance ($\sigma_x^2$) True National Average ($\bar{Y}$)
World A: Equal Groups No spread in sizes $x_1 = 50, \; \theta_1 = 50\%$
($25$ "Yes") 		$x_2 = 50, \; \theta_2 = 50\%$
($25$ "Yes") 		$0$ $\frac{25 + 25}{100} = 50\%$
World B: Unequal Groups Same average $\mu_x = 50$! $x_1 = 10, \; \theta_1 = 10\%$
($1$ "Yes") 		$x_2 = 90, \; \theta_2 = 90\%$
($81$ "Yes") 		$40^2 = 1600$ $\frac{1 + 81}{100} = 82\%$

Look at World B: the average group size is still $\mu_x = \frac{10 + 90}{2} = 50$, and the simple average of the two group rates is still $\frac{10\% + 90\%}{2} = 50\%$. Yet $82\%$ of the people in the nation say "Yes"!

Why? Because when group sizes vary ($\sigma_x^2 > 0$), most of the people are in the larger groups ($90$ out of $100$ people belong to Group 2). And our formula gives the exact number:

$$\bar{Y} = \underbrace{0.01(50)}_{0.50} + \underbrace{0.01\left(\frac{1600}{50}\right)}_{+0.32 \text{ correction}} = 0.82 \quad (82\%)$$

4. Three Cases to Consider

Case 1: Independence
$\beta = 0 \implies \bar{Y} = \alpha$

If group size $x_j$ has no relation to the response rate $\theta_j$, the correction disappears. Small and large groups share the same rate $\alpha$.

Case 2: Positive Slope
$\beta > 0 \implies \text{Shifts Up}$

If larger groups have higher rates $\theta_j$, the overall person-average is pulled above the average group rate by $+\beta \frac{\sigma_x^2}{\mu_x}$.

Case 3: Negative Slope
$\beta < 0 \implies \text{Shifts Down}$

If larger groups have lower rates $\theta_j$ (for example, more newcomers), the overall person-average is pulled below the average group rate.

5. Why Real-World Surveys Get Hard—And How a Hierarchical Structure Helps

At the end of this section in the book, there is a very honest observation about how hard this gets in practice:

"In general, the analysis of sample surveys with unequal sampling probabilities can be difficult because of the large number of potential categories, each with its own model for the distribution of $y$ given $x$ and a parameter for the frequency of that category in the population. At this point, hierarchical models may be needed to obtain good results." — Gelman et al., BDA3

Why does this become so difficult?

Think back to our causal diagram: a person's chance of ending up in the sample doesn't just depend on group size. It depends on a whole mix of factors—day of the week, weather, how often they attend, age, location, and personal circumstances. Once you slice the population across all of those factors, you end up with hundreds or thousands of small categories, and for every category you need to know two things:

  1. The Outcome Model $p(y \mid x)$: How people in that specific category respond to the survey.
  2. The Population Frequency $p(x)$: How common that category actually is in the broader population.

If we try to estimate every category on its own, the data thins out immediately—most cells will have only one or two people in the sample, or none at all.

This is where the hierarchical structure really helps: instead of estimating each group or category in isolation, a hierarchical model shares information across them through partial pooling. Even when individual categories are sparse or unobserved, the shared population distribution $(\mu_x, \sigma_x, \alpha, \beta, \sigma_\theta)$ keeps the estimates grounded and lets us piece together a sensible estimate for the whole population.
