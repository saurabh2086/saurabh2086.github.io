First-Principles Derivation

The Product Rule

Section 2.1 asks a simple but deep question: how should the plausibility of two truths together be built from the plausibility of each one, taken one step at a time? The answer is the product rule, and it is the first quantitative bridge from logic to inference.

1. Setup — the three demands

“Probability theory is nothing but common sense reduced to calculation.” — Laplace, 1819

Before we can combine beliefs, we have to say what a “belief” even is and what we are allowed to demand of it. Jaynes does something bold here: he refuses to assume any rule of probability. Instead he writes down a few requirements so reasonable you would nod at each one, and then shows that those requirements force the rules of probability into existence. This section lays out those requirements. Everything in the rest of the chapter is squeezed out of them.

1.1 The robot and its job

Jaynes asks us to imagine a reasoning robot. Not because we care about machines, but because a robot has no ego, no wishful thinking, and no memory of what it hoped would be true. It reasons only from what it has been told. That makes it the perfect stand-in for “reasoning done right.”

The robot's one job is to assign, to every proposition, a number saying how plausible that proposition is given whatever it currently knows. Nothing is judged in a vacuum: every plausibility rides on a background of information. We write this with a vertical bar meaning “given”:

$$ (A \mid C) \;=\; \text{the plausibility of } A, \text{ given the background knowledge } C. $$

Our task in Chapter 2 is not to compute any particular plausibility, but to discover the rules these numbers must obey so that the robot never reasons itself into nonsense.

1.2 The three desiderata

Jaynes states three broad demands — he calls them desiderata, the things we desire of any honest reasoning system. Read each one and notice how ordinary it sounds.

Desideratum I — Representation by real numbers

Degrees of plausibility are represented by real numbers. Not “pretty likely” or “a hunch” — an actual number, so that we can eventually calculate with them and compare any two.

Desideratum II — Qualitative agreement with common sense

The numbers must move the way common sense moves. If new information makes a proposition more plausible, its number must go up, never down — and it must do so continuously, with no sudden jumps. A whisker of evidence cannot flip a conclusion by a mile.

Desideratum III — Consistency

The robot must never contradict itself. Jaynes splits this into three parts:

(IIIa) If a conclusion can be reached in more than one way, every route must give the same answer.

(IIIb) The robot always uses all the relevant evidence it has — it ignores nothing.

(IIIc) Equivalent states of knowledge must get equivalent plausibilities — the robot's conclusions depend on what it knows, not on how the question happened to be worded.

Of these, (IIIa) — “different routes must agree” — is the quiet giant. It looks harmless, but it is the demand that will do almost all the work of pinning down the product rule.

1.3 What we are about to prove

That is the entire toolkit: numbers, common sense, consistency. No formula for probability has been assumed — we have only stated what any sane reasoner must satisfy. The astonishing claim of this chapter is that these three demands are enough. From them, the rule for combining the plausibility of “$A$ and $B$” is not one option among many; it is the only possibility that survives.

Here is the road ahead. We first ask how to judge two propositions at once (the two-step picture), rule out the tempting-but-broken shortcuts, and write the honest form $(AB\mid C) = F[(B\mid C),(A\mid BC)]$. Common sense then forces the combining recipe $F$ to be smooth and rising. Consistency across three propositions forces it to satisfy a famous functional equation. Solving that equation makes a rescaling function $w$ appear, which turns the whole mystery into ordinary multiplication — the product rule. Finally we pin the scale so that certainty is $1$ and impossibility is $0$.

Walk-away line

We assume nothing about probability — only that beliefs are numbers, agree with common sense, and never contradict themselves. Those three demands are the seeds from which the entire product rule grows.

2. The question: judging “A and B”

With the three demands in hand, we ask the first real question of the chapter. How plausible is it that two propositions are true together? Write “$A$ and $B$ are both true” — the logical AND — simply as $AB$. We want the plausibility of $AB$ given the background $C$, which we write $(AB \mid C)$. Everything that follows grows out of one plain observation about how a mind actually reaches such a judgment.

2.1 You cannot judge a conjunction in one gulp

Picture a stranger walking toward you, and suppose you want to judge one claim about them: black hair and blue eyes. Notice what your mind actually does. It does not weigh “black-hair-and-blue-eyes” as a single lump. It proceeds in steps.

First it asks: does this person have black hair? Suppose you look and — yes, black hair. Then, holding that as settled, it asks the second question: given that the hair is black, how plausible are blue eyes? That is two steps, and crucially the second step is judged inside the world where the first has already come true.

$$ \underbrace{(B \mid C)}_{\text{step 1: is }B\text{ true?}} \qquad\longrightarrow\qquad \underbrace{(A \mid BC)}_{\text{step 2: is }A\text{ true, given }B?} $$

Read the second piece carefully. It is not “how plausible are blue eyes in general.” It is blue eyes in the world where the hair is already known to be black. That conditioning is the whole reason the two-step view is more powerful than it first appears — and section 3 will show it is exactly what a broken shortcut leaves out.

2.2 The two orders, and why they must agree

Nothing forced us to check the hair first. We could just as well have checked the eyes first, and then — taking blue eyes as settled — asked about the hair. That is a second, equally valid route:

$$ \text{Route 1:}\quad (B \mid C)\ \text{then}\ (A \mid BC) \qquad\qquad \text{Route 2:}\quad (A \mid C)\ \text{then}\ (B \mid AC) $$

Both routes judge the very same thing — the plausibility of $AB$ — because “$A$ and $B$” is the same claim as “$B$ and $A$”. So whichever way the robot walks, it had better arrive at the same number. This is not an extra assumption; it is simply desideratum (IIIa) from the Setup: if a conclusion can be reached in more than one way, every way must agree.

Keep this in view. It looks like a modest bookkeeping remark, but “both orders must agree” is the loaded spring of the entire derivation. In section 4 we will press on it with a third proposition, and out of it the shape of the product rule will pop.

Walk-away line

To judge “$A$ and $B$,” you never judge it directly — you judge one, then the other given the first, and because the two orders describe the same claim, they must land on the same number.

3. Which ingredients do we actually need?

We have the two-step picture. Now the sharp question: which plausibilities do those steps actually require? Getting this right is what separates a rule that can reason like a human from one that cannot.

3.1 What you need, and what you can throw away

Going down Route 1, you need two things: $(B \mid C)$ — how plausible $B$ is — and $(A \mid BC)$ — how plausible $A$ is once $B$ is granted. What you do not need is $(A \mid C)$, the standalone plausibility of $A$ ignoring $B$. Why not? If $B$ turns out false, then $AB$ is already dead no matter what $A$ is doing; and if $B$ is true, you have moved into the conditioned world and want $(A \mid BC)$ instead. The plain $(A \mid C)$ never gets used — it would tell you nothing extra.

Jaynes sharpens this with a memorable point. Suppose the robot learns that the earth is round, and is now reasoning about cosmology. Should it drag back in the opinions it would have held if it did not know the earth is round? Of course not. Knowing a fact shrinks the world of possibilities you must carry. Once $C$ is known, only plausibilities conditioned on $C$ are ever relevant; the “what if I didn't know $C$” versions are dead weight.

3.2 The trap: why the marginals alone fail

Here is the shortcut that seems reasonable and is fatally wrong. Why not build the joint plausibility out of the two standalone judgments — $A$ given $C$ and $B$ given $C$ — with no conditioning of one on the other?

$$ (AB \mid C) \overset{?}{=} F\big[(A \mid C),\, (B \mid C)\big] \qquad \text{— tempting, but broken.} $$

Watch it break. Pick a random person and consider their eyes:

It is quite plausible the left eye is blue. It is quite plausible the right eye is brown. But both at once? Almost impossible — a person's eyes match. Each piece alone is believable, yet the conjunction is nearly ruled out. A recipe that only ever sees the two alone numbers receives identical inputs whether the two facts reinforce each other (black hair, blue eyes) or clash (blue eye, brown eye). It literally cannot tell the difference.

The lesson

To judge $A$ and $B$ together you need at least one conditional ingredient — a plausibility that already knows about the other proposition. That piece, $(A \mid BC)$, is what carries whether the two facts help each other or fight.

3.3 The honest equation

So we commit only to what we are sure of: the joint plausibility is built from those two pieces, by some combining recipe whose shape we do not yet know. Call the recipe $F$ and write it down — this is Jaynes's equation (2.1):

Equation (2.1) — the form of the product rule
$$ (AB \mid C) = F\big[(B \mid C),\, (A \mid BC)\big] $$

Read plainly: plausibility of both = some recipe $F$ applied to (plausibility of $B$) and (plausibility of $A$ given $B$). Note what we have not said. We have not claimed $F$ is multiplication, or addition, or anything at all beyond “two inputs, one output.” That restraint is deliberate: we will let the three demands tell us what $F$ must be, rather than guessing.

Is two ingredients really the right choice, though? Jaynes does not just assert it. Following Tribus, one can list every plausible way to build the joint out of the available numbers — there turn out to be eleven candidate recipes — and stress-test each against extreme cases like the blue-and-brown eyes. Nine of the eleven collapse: they violate common sense in some corner, exactly as the marginal-only shortcut did. Only two survive:

$$ (AB \mid C) = F\big[(B\mid C),\,(A\mid BC)\big] \qquad\text{or}\qquad (AB \mid C) = F\big[(A\mid C),\,(B\mid AC)\big] $$

and those two are nothing but our two-step story told in its two orders. Nothing new sneaked in. The picture from section 2 was right, and it has now survived a brute-force search.

Walk-away line

The joint plausibility is some two-input recipe $F\big[(B\mid C),(A\mid BC)\big]$ — we have named the recipe without yet knowing its shape, and a brute-force check confirms this is the only sensible form.

4. Common sense constrains the recipe

The recipe $F$ is still a total mystery: two inputs, one output, no shape. But it cannot be just any function. Desideratum II — qualitative agreement with common sense — quietly rules out almost all of them. Two demands do the work, and both are obvious once stated.

4.1 It must rise, never fall

Let us state this precisely, the way Jaynes does. Suppose the prior information changes, $C \to C'$, in a way that makes $B$ more plausible while leaving the second-step judgment $A \mid B$ untouched:

$$ B \mid C' > B \mid C, \tag{2.4} $$ $$ A \mid BC' = A \mid BC. \tag{2.5} $$

What should happen to the plausibility of “$A$ and $B$ together”? It can only go up, or at worst stay flat — never down. More faith in $B$ producing less faith in “both” would be nonsense:

$$ AB \mid C' \ge AB \mid C, \tag{2.6} $$

The inequality is usually strict (“both” genuinely rises), but there is one exception where it stays flat — the “equality” case. Here is why. Suppose $A$ is impossible given $B$, that is, $A \mid BC$ represents impossibility. Then even if $B$ becomes certain, $A$ still cannot be true — so “$A$ and $B$” is impossible no matter what. Pushing $B$'s plausibility higher does nothing, because the joint was already pinned at the bottom. That is the only situation in which raising an input leaves the output unchanged.

The same must hold for the other input. Take prior information $C''$ that leaves $B$ untouched but makes $A \mid B$ more plausible:

$$ B \mid C'' = B \mid C, \tag{2.7} $$ $$ A \mid BC'' > A \mid BC. \tag{2.8} $$

Then again the joint can only rise or hold:

$$ AB \mid C'' \ge AB \mid C, \tag{2.9} $$

and by the mirror-image reasoning, the flat case here needs $B$ to be impossible given $C$: if there is no $B$ to speak of, then “$A$ and $B$” is impossible regardless of how plausible $A$ becomes, so raising $A \mid B$ cannot lift it. Put together, these say that $F$ never decreases when you increase either input — it is monotonic increasing in both of its slots.

Why “equality” means something is impossible

Raising an input normally raises the joint. The only way the joint can stay put is if it is already stuck at the bottom — impossibility — and nothing can lift it. That happens exactly when one of the two propositions cannot occur: an impossible $A$ (given $B$) or an impossible $B$ (given $C$) makes “$A$ and $B$” impossible on its own, so the other input becomes irrelevant. Impossibility is a floor; you cannot push something lower than the floor, and while you are on it, pushing the other lever does nothing.

4.2 No jumps

Now imagine a hair's-width increase in how plausible $B$ is. Should the plausibility of “both” be allowed to lurch — to jump a large amount off that microscopic nudge? Clearly not. A whisker more evidence must not swing your conclusion by a mile. Tiny cause, tiny effect. That property is continuity: no cliffs, no sudden jumps.

Turn that picture into the shape of a statement. Nudge the first input by a small amount $\delta$ and watch the output. Continuity demands that as the nudge shrinks toward nothing, the change it causes in the joint shrinks toward nothing too:

$$ \lim_{\delta \to 0} F(x + \delta,\, y) = F(x, y), \qquad \lim_{\delta \to 0} F(x,\, y + \delta) = F(x, y). $$

Equivalently: a small $|\delta|$ forces a small $\big|F(x+\delta,y) - F(x,y)\big|$. This is exactly the condition Jaynes gives — for if $F$ were not continuous, an arbitrarily small increase in one of the plausibilities on the right-hand side of (2.1) could produce a large jump in $(AB \mid C)$, and that is the cliff common sense forbids.

Why insist on it now? Because continuity is what buys us the machinery of the next section. A curve with no jumps has a well-defined slope at every point; a curve that leaps does not. So continuity is precisely the license to differentiate $F$ — the tool that will let consistency (section 5) pin down its shape. It is the second half of desideratum II, quietly setting up the calculus to come.

4.3 The constraint, compactly

Put the two demands together and an enormous amount of freedom has already died. Out of all conceivable two-input recipes, only the smooth, always-rising ones remain:

$F$ must be a continuous, monotonic-increasing function of both inputs

“Rising in an input” is the same as saying its slope in that direction is not negative. Writing $F_1$ for the slope with respect to the first slot and $F_2$ for the second, this reads as equations (2.10a–b):

$$ F_1 \equiv \frac{\partial F}{\partial x} \ge 0, \qquad F_2 \equiv \frac{\partial F}{\partial y} \ge 0. $$

Do not let the subscripts intimidate: $F_1$ just means “how fast $F$ changes as you wiggle the first input,” and $F_2$ the second. Push either input up, the output does not go down. Continuity is what lets us speak of these slopes at all — and it is the doorway to the real machinery of the next section.

Walk-away line

$F$ cannot be any old recipe: sanity forces it to always rise (never fall) when a piece gets more plausible, and to do so smoothly, with no sudden jumps.

5. The consistency hammer

So far $F$ is smooth and rising, but still shapeless. Now we fire the spring we set in section 2 — “both orders must agree” — but with a twist that makes it enormously more powerful. We bring in a third proposition.

5.1 A third proposition, and grouping that cannot matter

Ask for the plausibility that $A$, $B$, and $C$ are all true, given some background $D$ — that is, $(ABC \mid D)$. Logic does not care how we bundle the three, because conjunction is associative:

$$ (AB)\,C \;=\; A\,(BC). $$

“$(A$ and $B)$, and then $C$” is the very same claim as “$A$, and then $(B$ and $C)$.” It is the same tower of blocks whether you glue the bottom two first or the top two first. But our recipe $F$ only ever combines things two at a time, so to reach all three we must combine twice — and there are two ways to do the bundling. Each way is a legal computation of the same number, so by desideratum (IIIa) they must agree.

Bundle $B$ and $C$ first (treat $BC$ as a single proposition, then expand it):

$$ (ABC \mid D) = F\big[(BC\mid D),\,(A\mid BCD)\big] = F\big\{\,F[(C\mid D),(B\mid CD)],\;(A\mid BCD)\,\big\}. \tag{2.12a} $$

Or bundle $A$ and $B$ first and reason in the other order:

$$ (ABC \mid D) = F\big[(C\mid D),\,(AB\mid CD)\big] = F\big\{\,(C\mid D),\;F[(B\mid CD),(A\mid BCD)]\,\big\}. \tag{2.12b} $$

Same three propositions, same final belief, different bundling. Consistency insists the two expressions be identical — otherwise the robot's answer would depend on trivial bookkeeping.

5.2 The Associativity Equation

Now strip away the propositions and keep only the numbers flowing through $F$. Name the three inputs

$$ x = (C\mid D),\qquad y = (B\mid CD),\qquad z = (A\mid BCD). $$

Then (2.12a) is $F[F(x,y),z]$ and (2.12b) is $F[x,F(y,z)]$, and demanding they be equal for all $x,y,z$ gives a pure constraint on $F$ alone:

Equation (2.13) — the Associativity Equation
$$ F\big[F(x,y),\,z\big] = F\big[x,\,F(y,z)\big]. $$

Read it plainly: it must not matter which pair you combine first. That is the entire content. And here is the thrill — we did not import this equation from a textbook. It fell out of a single demand: do not contradict yourself when you regroup. Almost every recipe you could dream up fails it; only a very special family survives. Finding that family is the work of the next section.

Historical note — the equation's pedigree

The Associativity Equation is not new to Jaynes. It goes back to N. H. Abel (1826). In his monumental survey of functional equations, Aczél (1966) gives it that very name and lists 98 works that use or discuss it. Aczél derives the general solution without even assuming differentiability — but the proof runs eleven pages.

Jaynes instead follows the shorter route of R. T. Cox (1961), which assumes $F$ is differentiable — and this is exactly why we fought for continuity in section 4.2. Continuity earns us the right to take the derivatives that Cox's proof needs.

Walk-away line

Because “$A$ and $B$ and $C$” can be bundled two ways but must give one answer, $F$ has to satisfy the Associativity Equation — “it cannot matter which pair you combine first” — and that single demand nearly determines $F$ completely.

6. Solving it: where $w$ comes from

We must now solve the Associativity Equation. The full derivation is a few pages of calculus, so here is the deal: the main thread below is the idea, and it reads on its own. The step-by-step algebra sits in the collapsible “Under the hood” boxes — open them if you want every line, skip them if you just want the story.

6.1 The idea: multiplication in disguise

Look again at the equation we must satisfy:

$$ F[F(x,y),z] = F[x,F(y,z)]. $$

Where have you already met an operation that does not care how you group things? Multiplication. $(x\cdot y)\cdot z = x\cdot(y\cdot z)$ — group it however you like, same answer. So is ordinary addition. That is the clue: this equation is the signature of multiplication.

So here is the hunch. What if $F$ is multiplication, just wearing a disguise? What if the plausibility numbers are on the “wrong ruler,” and once we relabel each one with some function — call it $w$ — the combining step turns into plain multiplication?

The hope, in one line
$$ w\big(F(x,y)\big) = w(x)\,w(y). \tag{2.26} $$

If such a $w$ exists, associativity is automatic — because multiplication is already associative, and the messy $F$ simply inherits the good behaviour. The whole job of the algebra below is to prove that such a $w$ must exist, and is essentially unique.

6.2 From the equation to a clean ratio

The first move is to differentiate the associativity equation — legal, thanks to the continuity we secured in 4.2 — and squeeze it down to a single tidy condition, Jaynes's equation (2.17).

Under the hood — differentiate, eliminate, and name the ratio $G$

Introduce the two inner combinations and treat $x,y,z$ as independent:

$$ u \equiv F(x,y), \qquad v \equiv F(y,z), $$ $$ \text{so the equation reads}\quad F(x,v) = F(u,z). \tag{2.15} $$

The crucial bookkeeping: $u$ depends on $x$ and $y$; $v$ depends on $y$ and $z$ but not $x$; and $x,z$ are independent. Differentiate (2.15) with respect to $x$ (only $u$ moves on the right, and $v$ is frozen on the left):

$$ F_1(x,v) = F_1(u,z)\,F_1(x,y). \tag{2.16a} $$

Differentiate with respect to $y$ — now both $u$ and $v$ move, so each side has one term:

$$ F_2(x,v)\,F_1(y,z) = F_1(u,z)\,F_2(x,y). \tag{2.16b} $$

Eliminate the awkward $F_1(u,z)$ using (2.16a), then divide through. Defining the ratio $G \equiv F_2/F_1$:

$$ G(x,v)\,F_1(y,z) = G(x,y), \qquad G(x,y)\equiv\frac{F_2(x,y)}{F_1(x,y)}. \tag{2.17} $$

Notice the right side has no $z$ in it — yet the left side seems to contain $z$ through $v$ and $F_1(y,z)$. That tension is exactly what the next box exploits.

6.3 The mixed-partials trick

Since the right side of (2.17) carries no $z$, the left side's $z$-dependence must secretly cancel. Pushing on that fact — using the fact that mixed second derivatives commute — forces $G$ into a very rigid shape, equation (2.19).

Under the hood — why $G(x,y) = r\,H(x)/H(y)$

Multiply (2.17) by $G(y,z)=F_2(y,z)/F_1(y,z)$; the $F_1(y,z)$ cancels to give a twin equation:

$$ G(x,v)\,F_2(y,z) = G(x,y)\,G(y,z). \tag{2.18} $$

Name the two left sides. Let $U$ be the left side of (2.17) and $V$ the left side of (2.18), recalling $v = F(y,z)$:

$$ U \equiv G(x,v)\,F_1(y,z) = G(x,y), $$ $$ V \equiv G(x,v)\,F_2(y,z) = G(x,y)\,G(y,z). $$

Differentiate $U$ with respect to $z$. Both factors carry $z$ (through $v$, and directly in $F_1$), and $\partial v/\partial z = F_2(y,z)$, so by the product and chain rules:

$$ \frac{\partial U}{\partial z} = G_2(x,v)\,F_2(y,z)\,F_1(y,z) + G(x,v)\,\frac{\partial^2 F}{\partial z\,\partial y}. $$

Differentiate $V$ with respect to $y$. Here $\partial v/\partial y = F_1(y,z)$, so likewise:

$$ \frac{\partial V}{\partial y} = G_2(x,v)\,F_1(y,z)\,F_2(y,z) + G(x,v)\,\frac{\partial^2 F}{\partial y\,\partial z}. $$

Compare them. The first terms are identical because ordinary multiplication commutes, $F_2 F_1 = F_1 F_2$. The second terms are identical because mixed partial derivatives commute, $\partial^2 F/\partial z\,\partial y = \partial^2 F/\partial y\,\partial z$. Therefore:

$$ \frac{\partial U}{\partial z} = \frac{\partial V}{\partial y}. $$

Now cash it in. Since $U = G(x,y)$ contains no $z$, we have $\partial U/\partial z = 0$, and hence $\partial V/\partial y = 0$. But $V = G(x,y)\,G(y,z)$, so:

$$ G(x,y)\,G(y,z)\ \text{is independent of}\ y. $$

Now solve that condition. Fix the third variable at any convenient constant, $z = z_0$. Then $G(x,y)\,G(y,z_0)$ is still independent of $y$, so it is a function of $x$ alone — call it $\varphi(x)$:

$$ G(x,y)\,G(y,z_0) = \varphi(x). $$

Also, with $z_0$ frozen, $G(y,z_0)$ is a function of $y$ alone; name it $H(y) \equiv G(y,z_0)$. Rearranging the line above:

$$ G(x,y) = \frac{\varphi(x)}{H(y)}. \tag{$\ast$} $$

The function $\varphi$ is not independent — we pin it down by feeding $(\ast)$ back into itself. Equation $(\ast)$ holds for any pair of arguments, so put $y$ in the first slot and $z_0$ in the second:

$$ G(y,z_0) = \frac{\varphi(y)}{H(z_0)}. $$

But the left side is exactly $H(y)$ by definition. Hence $H(y) = \varphi(y)/H(z_0)$, which gives $\varphi(y) = H(y)\,H(z_0)$, i.e. $\varphi(x) = H(x)\,H(z_0)$. Since $z_0$ is a fixed constant, $H(z_0)$ is just a number; call it $r$. Substituting back into $(\ast)$:

$$ G(x,y) = \frac{\varphi(x)}{H(y)} = r\,\frac{H(x)}{H(y)}, \tag{2.19} $$

with $H$ an arbitrary function and $r=H(z_0)$ a constant — and $\varphi$ has vanished, since it was never independent of $H$. Two checks: substituting back, $G(x,y)G(y,z) = r^2\,H(x)/H(z)$, indeed free of $y$; and $G(y,z_0) = rH(y)/H(z_0) = H(y)$, matching the definition. Monotonicity from section 4 makes $G>0$, so $r>0$ and $H$ keeps one sign.

6.4 Integrate, and $w$ appears

Feed that rigid shape back in, and the pieces of $F$ become simple ratios of $H$. Their total differential integrates cleanly — and the act of integrating $1/H$ is precisely what defines the relabeling function $w$.

Under the hood — the total differential and the definition of $w$

Substituting (2.19) back into (2.17) and (2.18) recovers the two slopes of $F$:

$$ F_1(y,z) = \frac{H(v)}{H(y)}, \tag{2.20} $$ $$ F_2(y,z) = r\,\frac{H(v)}{H(z)}. \tag{2.21} $$

Since $v=F(y,z)$, its total differential $dv = F_1\,dy + F_2\,dz$ becomes, after dividing by $H(v)$:

$$ \frac{dv}{H(v)} = \frac{dy}{H(y)} + r\,\frac{dz}{H(z)}. \tag{2.22} $$

Every term has the form $dt/H(t)$. So define the function whose derivative is exactly $1/H$:

$$ w(x) \equiv \exp\!\left\{\int^{x}\frac{dx}{H(x)}\right\} \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad d\big[\ln w(t)\big] = \frac{dt}{H(t)}. \tag{2.24} $$

Integrating (2.22) and exponentiating (the constant of integration becomes an overall multiplicative factor):

$$ w[F(y,z)] = w(y)\,w(z)^{\,r}. \tag{2.23} $$

There is $w$ — the relabeling that turns the mysterious recipe $F$ into a product. Combining two beliefs has become multiplying their $w$-values. Only one blemish remains: the stray exponent $r$.

6.5 Killing the exponent: why $r = 1$

We feed the near-solution (2.23) back into the original associativity equation one last time. First, note that (2.23) holds for any pair of arguments, so we may write it in the general form

$$ w\big[F(a,b)\big] = w(a)\,w(b)^{\,r} \qquad\text{for any } a, b. \tag{2.23$'$} $$

Step 1 — apply $w$ to both sides of the associativity equation. Recall the equation we have been solving all along, $F(x,v) = F(u,z)$, with the inner combinations $u = F(x,y)$ and $v = F(y,z)$. Both sides are equal numbers, so applying $w$ keeps them equal:

$$ w\big[F(x,v)\big] = w\big[F(u,z)\big]. $$

Step 2 — expand each side once with (2.23$'$). Treat the left side as $F$ of the pair $(x,v)$ and the right side as $F$ of the pair $(u,z)$:

$$ w(x)\,w(v)^{\,r} = w(u)\,w(z)^{\,r}. \tag{i} $$

Step 3 — expand $u$ and $v$ a second time. They are themselves outputs of $F$, so (2.23$'$) applies to each. For $v = F(y,z)$:

$$ w(v) = w\big[F(y,z)\big] = w(y)\,w(z)^{\,r} \quad\Longrightarrow\quad w(v)^{\,r} = \big[w(y)\,w(z)^{\,r}\big]^{\,r} = w(y)^{\,r}\,w(z)^{\,r^2}. $$

And for $u = F(x,y)$:

$$ w(u) = w\big[F(x,y)\big] = w(x)\,w(y)^{\,r}. $$

Step 4 — substitute both back into (i). The left side of (i) becomes

$$ w(x)\,w(v)^{\,r} = w(x)\,w(y)^{\,r}\,w(z)^{\,r^2}, $$

and the right side of (i) becomes

$$ w(u)\,w(z)^{\,r} = w(x)\,w(y)^{\,r}\,w(z)^{\,r}. $$

Setting them equal gives Jaynes's equation (2.25):

$$ w(x)\,w(y)^{\,r}\,w(z)^{\,r^2} = w(x)\,w(y)^{\,r}\,w(z)^{\,r}. \tag{2.25} $$

Step 5 — cancel and read off $r$. Because $w$ is positive, the common factor $w(x)\,w(y)^{\,r}$ is nonzero and may be divided out from both sides:

$$ w(z)^{\,r^2} = w(z)^{\,r}. $$

This must hold for every proposition $z$. Since $w$ is monotonic and non-constant, $w(z)$ takes a whole range of values, so we may pick a $z$ with $w(z) \neq 1$ and divide:

$$ w(z)^{\,r^2 - r} = 1 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad r^2 - r = 0 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad r(r-1) = 0 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad r = 0 \ \text{or}\ r = 1. $$

The root $r = 0$ would make $G = r\,H(x)/H(y) = 0$, collapsing $F$ to a constant and destroying the monotonicity we insisted on in section 4 — the useless trivial solution. That leaves the single admissible value:

$$ r = 1. $$

With $r=1$, equation (2.23) collapses to the clean form we hoped for back in 6.1:

Equations (2.26)–(2.27)
$$ w[F(x,y)] = w(x)\,w(y) \qquad\Longleftrightarrow\qquad F(x,y) = w^{-1}\!\big[w(x)\,w(y)\big]. $$

The mysterious recipe $F$ is nothing but multiplication on the $w$-ruler — no stray exponent, no other freedom. That is the whole content of the Associativity Equation, unpacked.

Walk-away line

Solving the Associativity Equation makes a relabeling $w$ appear that turns “combine two beliefs” into “multiply their $w$-values” — and feeding the near-solution back into itself forces the last exponent to $1$, leaving pure multiplication.

7. The product rule

7.1 Reading the result back into propositions

Equation (2.26) is stated in the bare variables $x$ and $y$. But we know what those stood for: back in section 3 the two ingredients were $x = (B\mid C)$ and $y = (A\mid BC)$. Put them back, and $F(x,y)$ is just $(AB\mid C)$. So (2.26) becomes the headline result of the chapter, Jaynes's equation (2.28):

Equation (2.28) — the product rule
$$ w(AB \mid C) = w(A \mid BC)\,w(B \mid C) = w(B \mid AC)\,w(A \mid C). $$

Read the first form out loud: the (relabeled) plausibility of “$A$ and $B$” equals the plausibility of $A$ given $B$, times the plausibility of $B$. The mysterious two-step combining of section 2 has turned out to be nothing but multiplication, once belief is measured on the $w$-ruler. The second form is the very same product computed in the other order — exactly the “both orders must agree” requirement from section 2, now written as an identity.

One more thing is worth saying. We derived (2.28) as a necessary condition for consistency. It is also sufficient: once the product rule holds, any joint plausibility of any number of propositions — say $(ABCDEFG \mid H)$ — can be expanded by partitioning in a great many different orders, and every one of them yields the same answer. Consistency is not just satisfied in the three-proposition case we used; it is secured for good.

7.2 Check it by hand

The whole apparatus was built so that regrouping never changes the answer. Let us watch that happen. On the $w$-ruler, combining is multiplication, so building “$A$ and $B$ and $C$” both ways gives:

$$ w\big[F(F(x,y),z)\big] = w(x)\,w(y)\,w(z) = w\big[F(x,F(y,z))\big]. $$

Both groupings collapse to the same product $w(x)\,w(y)\,w(z)$, because plain multiplication does not care how you bracket it. That is the entire point of the derivation, seen in a single line.

And with actual numbers, to count it on your fingers. Suppose the three relabeled beliefs are each $\tfrac12$:

$$ \big(\tfrac12 \cdot \tfrac12\big)\cdot \tfrac12 = \tfrac14 \cdot \tfrac12 = \tfrac18, \qquad \tfrac12 \cdot \big(\tfrac12 \cdot \tfrac12\big) = \tfrac12 \cdot \tfrac14 = \tfrac18. $$

Same answer, $\tfrac18$, either way. Regrouping is invisible — precisely what the consistency hammer of section 5 demanded, and precisely what $r=1$ delivered.

Walk-away line

The strange “combine two beliefs” recipe is just multiplication in disguise: relabel each belief with $w$, and “belief in $A$ and $B$” is “belief in $A$ given $B$” times “belief in $B$.” That is the product rule — forced, not assumed.

8. Fixing the scale

The product rule is proved, but $w$ is still floating: we never said what number stands for “certain” or for “impossible.” Common sense — desideratum II again — pins both ends, using the very rule we just earned.

8.1 Certainty forces $w = 1$

Suppose $A$ is certain given $C$. Then “$A$ and $B$” is really just “$B$” — the $A$ part adds nothing, since it is guaranteed. Whenever $B$ is true, $AB$ is true, and vice versa. Two propositions with the same truth value must have the same plausibility (the most primitive axiom of all), so:

$$ AB \mid C = B \mid C, \tag{2.29} $$ $$ A \mid BC = A \mid C. \tag{2.30} $$

Feed these into the product rule $w(AB\mid C) = w(A\mid BC)\,w(B\mid C)$:

$$ w(B \mid C) = w(A \mid C)\,w(B \mid C). \tag{2.31} $$

This must hold no matter what $B$ is — for every value $w(B\mid C)$ could take. The only way $w(B\mid C) = w(A\mid C)\cdot w(B\mid C)$ can survive for all $B$ is if the multiplier is exactly one:

Equation (2.32) — certainty
$$ \text{certainty is represented by}\quad w(A \mid C) = 1. $$

8.2 Impossibility forces $w = 0$ (or $\infty$)

Now suppose $A$ is impossible given $C$. Then “$A$ and $B$” is impossible too, right alongside $A$; and $A$ stays impossible whatever compatible $B$ you might learn:

$$ AB \mid C = A \mid C, \tag{2.33} $$ $$ A \mid BC = A \mid C. \tag{2.34} $$

The product rule then gives:

$$ w(A \mid C) = w(A \mid C)\,w(B \mid C). \tag{2.35} $$

Again this must hold for every $B$. Write $m = w(A\mid C)$ for the impossibility value; then $m = m\,w(B\mid C)$ for all values of $w(B\mid C)$. If $m$ were any ordinary nonzero, finite number, this would demand $w(B\mid C) = 1$ always — absurd. Only two escapes remain:

$$ w(A \mid C) = 0 \qquad\text{or}\qquad w(A \mid C) = +\infty. $$

Both “absorb” multiplication ($0\cdot\text{anything}=0$, and $\infty$ swamps anything). The choice $-\infty$ is ruled out: by continuity it would force some $w$-values negative, contradicting (2.35).

8.3 Why $0$-to-$1$ is a free convention

So $w$ is a positive, continuous, monotonic function that can run two ways: increasing, from $0$ for impossibility up to $1$ for certainty; or decreasing, from $\infty$ for impossibility down to $1$ for certainty. Which should we pick?

It genuinely does not matter — the two carry identical content. Given any decreasing $w_1$ with impossibility at $\infty$, simply define

$$ w_2(x) \equiv \frac{1}{w_1(x)}, $$

which is increasing, puts impossibility at $0$, and obeys all the same rules. Same theory, relabeled. So we choose the familiar one purely by convention:

Convention
$$ 0 \le w(x) \le 1, \qquad \text{impossible} = 0, \quad \text{certain} = 1. $$

This is the familiar probability scale — arrived at last, and chosen only after proving the choice costs nothing.

Walk-away line

The product rule itself forces certainty to be $1$ and impossibility to be $0$ (or its mirror image $\infty$), so the $0$-to-$1$ scale is a convenience we adopt, not an assumption we make.

9. Why it matters, and what comes next

9.1 What we actually proved

Step back and look at the whole climb. We began with three demands so mild that anyone would grant them: beliefs should be real numbers, they should move the way common sense moves, and they should never contradict themselves. We assumed nothing about probability. And yet, one forced step at a time, those demands delivered a single, inescapable rule for combining the plausibility of “$A$ and $B$”:

The product rule, in the notation you already know
$$ P(AB \mid C) = P(A \mid BC)\,P(B \mid C). $$

Retrace the chain: the two-step picture (§2) $\to$ the honest form $F[(B\mid C),(A\mid BC)]$ and the eye-color trap (§3) $\to$ common sense makes $F$ smooth and rising (§4) $\to$ consistency across three propositions gives the Associativity Equation (§5) $\to$ solving it births $w$ and turns $F$ into multiplication (§6) $\to$ the product rule (§7) $\to$ the scale is pinned to $[0,1]$ (§8). At no point did we get to choose the rule; each step closed off every alternative. That is the deep lesson: the product rule is not one convention among many. It is the only way to combine beliefs without eventually contradicting yourself.

This is what Laplace's epigraph really means. Probability is not a separate axiom system bolted onto logic; it is what ordinary consistent reasoning becomes once you insist on carrying it out with numbers.

9.2 What comes next: the sum rule

The product rule tells us how to handle “$A$ and $B$.” But a complete calculus of belief needs one more piece: how does the plausibility of $A$ relate to the plausibility of its negation, “not $A$”? Section 2.2 attacks exactly that, in the very same spirit — write down a functional relation $v = S(u)$ between a belief and its opposite, demand consistency with the product rule we just built, and watch it force a unique answer.

That answer is the sum rule, $P(A\mid C) + P(\bar A\mid C) = 1$, and together with the product rule it completes the foundation. Everything else in Bayesian inference — Bayes' theorem, marginalization, updating on evidence — is bookkeeping on top of these two.

One-line summary

Demand only that beliefs be numbers, agree with common sense, and never contradict themselves — and the product rule of probability is the only thing that can result.
